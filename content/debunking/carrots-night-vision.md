In 1940, the Royal Air Force started shooting down German bombers in the dark at rates that didn't fit their daytime numbers. The reason was secret: a new system called Airborne Interception radar, mounted in their night fighters, that let pilots find targets they couldn't see with their eyes.

To explain the sudden accuracy without revealing the radar, the British government told the press its pilots were eating large quantities of carrots. The carrots, they said, were giving them exceptional night vision. The Ministry of Information ran posters encouraging civilians to eat more carrots — partly to support the cover story, partly because carrots were one of the few foods not rationed during the war.

Eight decades later, the cover story has outlived the war by a wide margin. People still believe it. Children are still told to eat their carrots for their eyes.

The truth is more interesting than the propaganda.

## What Carrots Actually Do

Carrots contain beta-carotene, which the body converts into vitamin A. Vitamin A is genuinely important for vision — specifically for the production of rhodopsin, the pigment in the rods of your retina that lets you detect low light.

If you are vitamin A deficient, your night vision suffers. Severe deficiency causes a condition called nyctalopia — night blindness — that can be reversed by restoring vitamin A levels.

This is where the kernel of plausibility lives. If your diet lacks vitamin A, carrots will help. The propaganda took a real mechanism and stretched it into a false promise: that adding more carrots beyond what your body needs would keep improving your vision.

That isn't how vitamin A works.

## Why More Is Not Better

Vitamin A is a fat-soluble vitamin. Your body stores it in the liver. Once your stores are adequate, additional vitamin A doesn't enhance vision — it just accumulates. In high enough doses, it becomes toxic, causing symptoms ranging from headaches to liver damage to bone problems.

For most people in countries with reasonable food access, vitamin A deficiency is rare. You are almost certainly not deficient. Eating more carrots will not give you superhuman night vision. It will give you slightly higher vitamin A stores than you already had, which does nothing visible.

In fact, eating so many carrots that your skin turns orange — a condition called carotenemia, which actually happens — does not improve your eyesight either. It just makes you orange.

## How the Myth Persists

The carrot myth is unusual in being traceable to a single source. Most folk beliefs about food have murky origins. This one was deliberately manufactured by a specific government for a specific reason in a specific year, and we have the documents.

What's striking is how durable it became. The radar was declassified. The cover story was acknowledged. Historians have written about it. And the belief just kept going, passed from parents to children, because once a claim is good enough to repeat, the source stops mattering. The carrot story is simple, plausible-sounding, and tied to a virtue ("eat your vegetables"). It survives because it does work people want it to do.

That's a more general pattern worth noticing. False beliefs don't usually persist because the evidence keeps confirming them. They persist because they're useful as social tools — they back up things adults want to tell children, or simplify decisions, or fit into a story that already feels right.

If you want better night vision, what actually works is letting your eyes adapt for 20 to 30 minutes in the dark, avoiding bright light during that adaptation, and not staring at your phone screen if you want to keep it. Your rhodopsin will do its job. You don't need to eat anything orange.

## The Cover Story That Outlived the War

The radar that won those nighttime engagements is now a museum artifact. The pilots who used it are mostly gone. The Ministry of Information is no longer running propaganda about root vegetables.

The carrots are still on the plate, and the story is still on the lips, and that's the part that's actually interesting — not whether carrots help you see, but how long a useful lie can outlive the situation that made it useful.
