You've heard it, and you've probably passed it on: shave a patch of hair — a leg, a face, an arm — and it comes back darker, coarser, faster. It sounds like the kind of thing you can check for yourself. That's exactly why it survives.

The science isn't close. In 1928, the anatomist Mildred Trotter ran a controlled test — men shaving patches of facial hair under fixed conditions while she measured what grew back. No change: not in rate, not in thickness, not in colour. In 1970, dermatologists repeated it more carefully in the *Journal of Investigative Dermatology*, shaving volunteers' legs and comparing shaved skin to unshaved over several months. Same answer. Every controlled study since has landed in the same place.

The reason is plain once you look below the surface. Shaving cuts the hair at the skin. It never touches the follicle — the living structure a few millimetres down that actually decides how thick, dark, and fast a hair grows. That machinery runs on hormones and genes, not on what you do to the dead shaft above the skin. A razor has no more say over the follicle than mowing a lawn has over its roots.

So why does the regrowth feel coarser? Because it genuinely does — to the touch and to the eye — while nothing about the hair has changed. Two things are happening.

A hair that grew in untouched tapers to a fine point. When you shave, you slice it flat, leaving a blunt cross-section at the full width of the shaft. Blunt stubble feels rougher against your hand than a soft tapered end, and it stands stiffer. Nothing thicker has grown; you've just removed the fine tip and left the fat middle.

And a blunt, flat end catches light differently than a fine point. It reads as darker — especially as short stubble against paler skin, before it has grown long enough to lie down, catch the sun, and lighten. The contrast sells the illusion completely.

Here's the part worth keeping. This myth is harder to kill than most, and not because people are careless. It's because your own senses seem to run the experiment and confirm it every single time. You shave; a few days later you feel the coarser stubble; the belief is "verified." But the illusion is baked into the observation itself. What you feel is real — the stubble really is blunter — but that's evidence about the shape of a cut, not about how the hair grew. The observation and the conclusion are two different things, and the myth quietly swaps one for the other.

That's a different kind of failure than most debunked claims. It doesn't need a rumour or a misread study to spread. It regenerates on its own, in each person, out of a true sensation wrongly explained. The fix isn't to trust your senses less — it's to be exact about what they're actually telling you. You felt blunter stubble. True. It does not follow that the follicle changed, and the only way to know is the one thing the razor can't show you: a controlled measurement. Which has been done, many times, with the same boring result.

Shave or don't. It won't bring the hair back any thicker than it was going to be — and if it looks like it did, that's the cut you're seeing, not the hair.
