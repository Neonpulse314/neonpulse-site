You've probably heard it at some point: the average person swallows eight spiders per year while sleeping. It circulates on "surprising facts" lists, gets shared at dinner parties, and turns up in trivia games. It feels specific enough to be real.

It isn't. There is no scientific basis for the claim, and basic facts about spider biology make it nearly impossible.

## Where the Claim Comes From

The origin of the "eight spiders" figure is murky, which is itself informative. For a supposedly established fact, it has no clear source — no study, no entomologist, no dataset. It appears to have spread through "fascinating facts" content in the early internet era without anyone tracing it to anything real.

One frequently cited origin is a 1993 column in *PC Professional* magazine by writer Lisa Holst, who supposedly listed plausible-sounding but false facts to demonstrate how credulously people share misinformation. The spider claim was allegedly one of her fabrications. But even this debunking origin story is disputed — the Holst column is difficult to verify independently, and some researchers have been unable to confirm it exists at all. The claim may have simply emerged from the noise of early internet chain emails.

What's consistent across every version: there is no study that counts swallowed spiders. No researcher has attempted to verify the figure. It has the form of a statistic without any of the substance.

## Why Spiders Don't Walk Into Sleeping Mouths

The claim becomes even less plausible when you understand how spiders behave.

Spiders are highly sensitive to vibration. They use vibrations to detect prey, predators, and mates — it's one of their primary sensory channels. A sleeping human is not a quiet environment. Even during normal sleep, a human body produces continuous vibrations: the chest rises and falls with breathing, the heart beats, and many people snore or shift position. From a spider's perspective, a sleeping person is an enormous vibrating object.

Spiders also have no reason to approach a human mouth. They are not searching for warmth (they're cold-blooded and regulate temperature by moving between environments). They are not attracted to breath. They are not looking for food inside a large mammal's face. An open mouth offers nothing that a spider would seek out and represents the kind of large-scale movement and vibration that spiders are wired to avoid.

When spiders do enter human living spaces, they are hunting — following prey like flies and small insects along walls and ceilings. The path of a hunting spider does not typically lead to the center of a bed, up a mattress, across a sleeping body, and into an open mouth.

None of this is to say a spider has *never* accidentally ended up somewhere unfortunate. Individual anomalous events happen. But an average of eight times per person per year would require a regular, reliable mechanism — and there isn't one.

## How Unlikely Events Become "Facts"

The spider claim illustrates a pattern in how misinformation spreads. A piece of information that is:

- **Specific** (not "sometimes" but "eight per year")
- **Viscerally memorable** (spiders, in your mouth, while you sleep)
- **Hard to disprove personally** (you're asleep)

...has a natural advantage over accurate but less vivid information. The specificity signals credibility — someone must have measured this. The visceral quality makes it stick. The unverifiability makes it hard to dismiss.

These three properties together create a claim that travels well regardless of whether it's true.

The mechanism is the same one that kept the tongue taste zone map in textbooks for fifty years, or that kept the claim that we only use 10% of our brains circulating for decades. The claims had the *form* of facts — specific, citable, memorable — without the substance.
