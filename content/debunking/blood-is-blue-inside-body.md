Hold your wrist up to a light. The veins visible beneath the skin look distinctly blue or blue-green. It seems obvious: inside your body, where there's no oxygen, blood must be blue.

It isn't. Your blood is never blue. Not when it's oxygenated, not when it's deoxygenated, not at any point in your circulatory system.

## What Deoxygenated Blood Actually Looks Like

Blood gets its color from hemoglobin, the protein in red blood cells that binds to oxygen. When hemoglobin is carrying oxygen — as it is in arteries heading out from the lungs — it's bright scarlet red. When it has released its oxygen to the tissues — as it is in veins heading back toward the lungs — it's a darker, more muted red. Not crimson. More like burgundy.

This is easy to verify. Blood drawn from a vein, which is deoxygenated, is visibly dark red. Surgeons see deoxygenated blood routinely. It is unmistakably red.

The myth likely persists in part because anatomy diagrams conventionally color arteries red and veins blue — a useful shorthand for telling them apart that gets misread as a description of actual blood color.

## Why Veins Look Blue

If the blood is dark red, why do veins appear blue through the skin?

The answer involves how light behaves when it passes through tissue.

Light isn't a single thing — it's a range of wavelengths. Different wavelengths penetrate the skin to different depths. Longer wavelengths (red, near-infrared) penetrate deeper into tissue. Shorter wavelengths (blue) scatter more at the surface and don't reach as far.

When light hits your skin above a vein, the red wavelengths travel deeper and are largely absorbed by the hemoglobin in the blood beneath. Blue wavelengths scatter near the surface and bounce back toward your eye. The result is that the vein appears blue — not because the blood is blue, but because the optical properties of skin and tissue filter the returning light in a way that emphasizes shorter wavelengths.

The same vein, seen directly during surgery rather than through skin, appears clearly dark red.

Skin tone affects this. In people with lighter skin, the filtering effect produces a distinctly blue appearance. In people with darker skin, veins may appear green or brown rather than blue, because the same light physics interacts differently with higher melanin concentrations. The blood is the same color in either case.

## The Oxygen Connection Is Real — Just Not the Color

It's worth being clear: the oxygenation difference is real. Oxygenated blood and deoxygenated blood genuinely look different. The difference is bright red versus dark red, not red versus blue. The mechanism that delivers that distinction — the change in hemoglobin's molecular structure when it binds and releases oxygen — is accurate biology. It just doesn't produce blue.

The body does contain one fluid that appears blue in at least some animals: hemocyanin, a copper-based oxygen carrier found in horseshoe crabs, octopuses, and some other invertebrates. Hemocyanin is colorless when deoxygenated and blue when oxygenated. Human blood doesn't contain it.

## Why the Myth Matters Slightly More Than It Seems

Most myths are harmless trivia. This one has a specific quirk: it sometimes shows up in explanations of medical conditions, in health education, and in how people understand their own bodies. A person who believes their blood is blue might be more confused — not less — when they see deoxygenated blood and find it unexpectedly red. The correction orients people to accurate biology, not just a fun fact.

It also illustrates a broader principle: what you observe is not always what's happening. The blue of your veins is a product of physics happening between the blood and your eyes — not a property of the blood itself. The gap between observation and reality is exactly where myths settle in, and exactly why the question "why does it look that way?" is always worth asking.
