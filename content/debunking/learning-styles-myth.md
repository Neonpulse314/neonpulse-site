If you went to school in the last three decades, you probably encountered learning styles. Maybe a teacher told you that you were a visual learner, or kinesthetic. Maybe you were given a quiz that assigned you a type. The idea is straightforward: different people take in information differently, and if your teaching matches your learning style, you'll understand better and remember more.

It's an appealing framework. It's also not supported by the research.

## The Claim

The most common version of learning styles theory is the VARK model, developed by Neil Fleming in the 1980s. VARK stands for Visual, Auditory, Reading/Writing, and Kinesthetic. The idea is that each person has a dominant mode — some people learn better through diagrams and visual information, others through hearing explanations, others through reading text, others through doing things with their hands.

The stronger version of the claim, called the "meshing hypothesis," goes further: students learn best when instruction is delivered in their dominant style. A visual learner taught through text underperforms. Teach them through diagrams and they'll do better.

This hypothesis is what's been tested. And it has consistently failed to hold up.

## What the Research Shows

A comprehensive review by Pashler and colleagues, published in *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* in 2008, examined the existing literature on learning styles and set out a clear standard: for the meshing hypothesis to be true, students classified as visual learners should show better outcomes when taught visually than when taught verbally, and vice versa. This is called a "crossover interaction" — the style that helps one group should hurt the other.

The review found no credible evidence of this pattern. Some studies showed that people have different preferences. None showed that matching instruction to those preferences improved learning.

Subsequent large-scale reviews reached similar conclusions. A 2018 meta-analysis published in *Anatomical Sciences Education* surveyed nearly 2,000 students, found that the majority identified with a learning style preference, and then found that matching study strategies to stated preferences had no relationship with academic outcomes. Students who "matched" their learning style didn't outperform students who didn't.

## Why Preferences Exist Without Learning Styles Being Real

This seems counterintuitive. If someone says they prefer visual information and genuinely enjoys visual presentation, why wouldn't they learn better from it?

The answer is that preference and optimal learning are different things.

For most subjects and most types of content, some forms of presentation are simply more effective than others — regardless of who the learner is. Spatial concepts like anatomy or geometry are learned better through visual representations for almost everyone. Sequential logic is often learned better through verbal explanation. The effectiveness of the presentation format is mostly a property of the content, not the learner.

What learners may be doing when they report a "learning style" is reporting what they enjoy, what feels comfortable, or what they're already good at. Those are real preferences. But learning often requires stepping outside what's comfortable — working at the edge of current understanding is where retention and comprehension improve. A preference for visual information doesn't mean the brain processes visual information better; it may mean visual information feels easier, which is not the same thing.

## Why the Framework Persisted

Learning styles theory has several properties that help ideas survive regardless of their accuracy.

It's categorizing and flattering. Being told you're a "visual learner" gives you an identity and an explanation for why you've sometimes found school difficult. It reframes struggle as misalignment rather than difficulty. That feels better than the alternative.

It's actionable for teachers. "Match your instruction to learning styles" is a concrete instructional strategy. Teachers want frameworks they can apply. Whether the framework is correct is secondary to whether it gives structure to practice.

It's intuitively plausible. Everyone has had the experience of a concept clicking when it was explained a different way. It's easy to generalize that into "I learn better visually" even when the specific case was just a better explanation.

The combination of intuitiveness, flattery, and practical application made learning styles durable in education long after the research turned against it. As of the early 2020s, surveys of teachers in multiple countries showed that the majority still believed learning styles were real and influenced their teaching practice.

## What Is True About How People Learn

Rejecting learning styles doesn't mean all learning is identical. Some things about learning are well-supported:

**Prior knowledge matters more than style.** What you already know about a topic is the strongest predictor of how quickly you'll learn more of it. Building connections to existing knowledge — regardless of presentation format — improves retention.

**Interleaving and spacing improve retention.** Spreading learning across multiple sessions and mixing related topics (rather than blocking all study of one topic before moving to the next) consistently produces better long-term recall.

**Retrieval practice outperforms re-reading.** Testing yourself on material, even before you feel ready, produces better retention than re-reading or reviewing. This is one of the most replicated findings in educational psychology.

None of these effects depend on learning style. They apply across learners, which is the opposite of what learning styles theory predicts.
