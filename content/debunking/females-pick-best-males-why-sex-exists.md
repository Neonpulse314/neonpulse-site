A reader sent me a hypothesis about why sexual reproduction exists. He'd been reading about the two-fold cost of sex and wrote: *the answer is because females pick the best available males, which speeds up evolution of good traits.*

It's a common place to land, and it's the kind of claim I usually take apart here for being false. This one isn't false. There are decades of beetles behind the mechanism.

It still doesn't answer the question he was asking — and the tell is something you can check yourself, in about thirty seconds, on any explanation of how anything got started.

## What actually got measured

Matt Gage's group at the University of East Anglia ran red flour beetles — *Tribolium castaneum* — through more than 45 generations of experimental evolution, about seven years. The populations differed in exactly one thing: how much sexual selection they experienced. At one end, strongly male-biased groups where many males competed for each female. At the other, enforced monogamy — one male, one female, no competition, nothing to choose between.

Then the lines were inbred to expose the genetic load each had accumulated. The result is the paper's title: *Sexual selection protects against extinction* (Lumley et al., **Nature** 522:470, 2015). Every weak-selection lineage was **extinct by the tenth generation** of brother–sister mating. Some strong-selection families were still going **past the twentieth**.

A 2026 follow-up took the same system out to 156 generations and sequenced whole genomes. Populations under strong sexual selection carried substantially fewer damaging variants — missense and nonsense mutations, counted directly. The control is what makes it worth quoting: nucleotide diversity and runs of homozygosity were similar across treatments, which rules out the boring explanation that strong selection had just shrunk the effective population and dragged everything down with it. The purge was aimed at damage, not general attrition.

The mechanism is close to what the reader guessed. Traits females select on are expensive to build, so a male carrying a heavy mutational load can't afford to fake them. Selecting against him removes those variants at low demographic cost — the losses land in the sex that isn't the limit on how many offspring the population can produce. That last part isn't the beetle group's observation; it's the formal argument from Agrawal's 2001 model, and we'll come back to it, because it's the whole ballgame.

## Two smaller corrections before the real one

**It subtracts bad; it doesn't demonstrably spread good.** The reader said "speeds up evolution of good traits." What was counted was *fewer damaging variants*. Nobody in these experiments measured beneficial alleles sweeping faster. Popular retellings blur both into "improves the gene pool," which lets a measured result stand in for a much harder claim.

**And the effect isn't reliable across species.** Arbuthnott and Rundle ran the equivalent test on *Drosophila melanogaster*, tracking six deleterious recessive alleles under high and low opportunity for sexual selection. Their title: *"Sexual selection is ineffectual or inhibits the purging of deleterious mutations"* (**Evolution** 66:2127, 2012). The literature since has gone both ways. The usual explanation for the flies isn't that the mechanism is absent but that **sexual conflict** — males harming females in the course of competing — eats the benefit. Which is a mechanism, not a coin flip, and it means the size of the effect depends on how nasty the mating system is.

Neither of those is fatal. This next one is structural.

## The bill

An asexual female puts all her offspring into daughters, every one of whom can bear young. A sexual female puts about half of hers into sons, who can't. So the sexual lineage runs at roughly half the reproductive rate — not once, but every generation, forever. John Maynard Smith formalised this in 1971, and it has been the obstacle ever since. (It assumes the asexual matches the sexual female's fecundity and that fathers contribute nothing to offspring survival; relax either and the factor of two shrinks.)

Here's the precision most pages skip. **The two-fold cost is not a cost of sex. It's a cost of males.** Isogamous species — those whose gametes are all the same size, with mating types rather than sexes, sometimes dozens or thousands of them — don't produce a class of individuals who can't bear offspring, so they never get this bill. They pay other costs: finding a partner, the risks of fusion, and the genome dilution of only passing on half your genes. But not this one. The two-fold factor arrives with **anisogamy**, the split into big gametes and small ones.

## Not two questions. Three.

Now the structure becomes visible, and it's the thing the popular claim flattens.

1. **Where did sex itself come from?** Gamete fusion and meiosis, in single-celled isogamous ancestors, well over a billion years ago. No males. No females. No two-fold cost.
2. **Where did males come from?** The split into large and small gametes. *This* is where the bill is born.
3. **Why does sex keep winning now?** An established sexual population resisting invasion by an asexual mutant. This is the question nearly all the evidence is about.

Ask "why does sex exist" and you are usually asking (1). Answer with female choice and you have answered (3).

And on (1), it isn't a near miss. Female choice among competing males has no referent at all in an isogamous ancestor. There are no males to choose between and no bill to pay. The explanation doesn't apply weakly there; it doesn't apply.

Question (2) is the honest complication, and I'd rather hand it to you than have you find it. Siller's 2001 model argues that variance in male mating success is ubiquitous and **likely preceded or evolved alongside anisogamy** — so "female choice needs males, therefore it came second" isn't the knockout it looks like. That ordering is genuinely open.

## What the proponents themselves called it

Which is why the strongest evidence for my point comes from the people making the opposite case.

In June 2001, *Nature* ran two papers back to back, both arguing that sexual selection could offset the two-fold cost by purging mutations where the losses are cheap:

- Siller, S., **"Sexual selection and the maintenance of sex"** — *Nature* 411:689
- Agrawal, A. F., **"Sexual selection and the maintenance of sexual reproduction"** — *Nature* 411:692

*Maintenance.* Both of them. These are the formal versions of the reader's idea, written by its most capable advocates, and they scoped it themselves — in the titles — to question (3).

Agrawal is even sharper about why the confusion is so tempting. Anisogamy is what creates the two-fold cost *and* what creates the conditions for sexual selection. Same source, both things. It is very easy to slide from "these arrived together" to "this one paid for that one." They arrived together. That is not the same as one having bought the other.

The chapter where Maynard Smith laid out the cost, incidentally, is called **"The Origin and Maintenance of Sex."** The distinction has been sitting in the title of the founding document the whole time. The field never lost it. The internet version did.

## The test, and why the obvious version of it is broken

The tempting rule to extract here is: *if an explanation presupposes the thing it's explaining, it can't explain how that thing started.* Write the explanation as a sentence, list what it needs to already exist, and see if the explanandum is on the list.

That rule is wrong, and it's worth seeing why, because the broken version will mislead you more often than it helps.

Try it on multicellularity. *"Multicellular life arose because cells that stuck together survived predators."* It needs cells sticking together. It's explaining cells sticking together. The rule fires — and the rule is wrong, because that's a leading origin account with direct experimental support: expose single-celled *Chlorella* to a predator that eats things below a certain size and clumps evolve in weeks. Same for autocatalytic chemistry, and for social conventions. **Origins of self-sustaining things are usually bootstraps.** They are supposed to contain a small version of themselves. Naive circularity convicts every one of them.

The repair is one question:

> **Is there a cheap version of the thing that's already available for free — enough to get the feedback started?**

That's the whole test.

- **Cells sticking together:** two daughter cells failing to separate costs approximately nothing and happens by accident, and a slightly-bigger clump already deters a gape-limited predator. Cheap seed exists. Legitimate bootstrap. ✅
- **A company that wins because it hires great people:** a small early success buys a slightly better hire, which buys a bit more success. Cheap seed exists — early Google and SpaceX hired brilliantly long before dominating anything. Legitimate bootstrap, and my first draft of this article got that one wrong in the other direction. ✅
- **Female choice as the reason sex exists:** at question (1) there's no cheap version, because there's no version at all — no males, nothing to choose. The benefit only switches on once the full two-fold bill is being paid. ❌

The refined test still isn't automatic: you can rig it by choosing a self-serving list of what counts as "cheap." What it does reliably is force the question into the open — *what's the smallest, cheapest form of this thing, and did the benefit exist at that size?* When the answer is "the benefit only appears at full price," you're holding a maintenance answer.

## So what does explain the origin?

Honestly: almost nothing, and I'd rather say that than fill the space.

The Red Queen — parasites coevolving against common genotypes — and recombination clearing mutational damage are the two hypotheses everyone quotes. They are also, both of them, **answers to question (3)**. Lively's snails, the *Daphnia* work, the whole empirical programme, is about established sexual populations resisting asexual invasion. Current thinking is pluralist: they aren't rivals, they patch each other's gaps. The Red Queen alone needs implausibly virulent parasites to work; add mutational load, which makes infected hosts sicker, and the virulence required drops to something realistic. Neither clears the bar alone.

But that whole conversation is downstream of question (1). The origin of meiosis and gamete fusion is a roughly two-billion-year-old event in organisms that left no fossils, and the last common ancestor of all eukaryotes was already sexual — so there's no surviving intermediate to look at. The serious candidates there are different in kind: sex as an outgrowth of DNA repair, and the possibility that meiosis was originally driven by selfish genetic elements rather than by any benefit to the host. Neither has much direct evidence, because there's very little direct evidence to be had.

That's the state of it. The question people most want answered is the one with the least data, and the confident answers in circulation are borrowed from the question next door.

The reader's idea was better than most of what I get sent. It named a real force, with a genome scan and decades of beetles behind it. It was filed under the wrong question — which is the most common way a good idea fails, and the hardest kind to catch, because every sentence in it is true.
