You know the rule. Eight glasses of water a day — sometimes six, sometimes two litres, sometimes a number a wellness app nudges you toward. It arrives with the authority of settled science. Miss your quota and you're "dehydrated": sluggish, foggy, ageing badly, whatever the caption says.

There is a real document behind the rule, and a physiologist who went looking for it.

## Where the number came from

In 2002 Heinz Valtin — a kidney specialist who had written two standard textbooks on water balance — set out to find where "8×8" came from. His best guess pointed back to 1945, when the Food and Nutrition Board of the US National Research Council published this:

> "A suitable allowance of water for adults is 2.5 liters daily in most instances. An ordinary standard for diverse persons is 1 milliliter for each calorie of food. Most of this quantity is contained in prepared foods."

Eight 8-ounce glasses comes to about 1.9 litres; the Board's figures — 2.5 litres, or its "1 millilitre per calorie" rule, roughly 2 litres for a typical diet — land in the same ballpark. Close enough that a number could stick. But read the third sentence: *most of this quantity is contained in prepared foods.* The Board was describing your **total** water intake — the water in soup, fruit, bread, coffee, everything — and noting that you already eat most of it.

Valtin's reading is that somewhere between 1945 and the bottle on your desk, that third sentence was overlooked. He's careful to say *may have*: he can't prove the chain of custody, only that it's the most plausible origin. But if he's right, a figure for total intake got read as a prescription for plain water you must drink *on top of* your food. The number didn't change. Its ending fell off, and the meaning flipped.

## No proof it was ever true

Tracing the origin was only half of what Valtin did. He also searched the literature for any study showing healthy people need that much plain water. His conclusion, published in the *American Journal of Physiology*: "I have found no scientific proof that absolutely every person must 'drink at least eight glasses of water a day.'"

Worse for the rule: surveys of thousands of healthy people showed their average fluid intake sitting *below* the 8×8 target, with no epidemic of dehydration to show for it. Valtin also killed a companion myth — that coffee and tea don't count because caffeine dries you out. In most people, caffeinated drinks add to your daily fluid; they don't subtract from it.

Five years later a *British Medical Journal* paper on medical myths (Vreeman and Carroll, 2007) put "you should drink at least eight glasses of water a day" on its list and reached the same verdict: no evidence behind it.

## You already have a controller

Here's the part the rule quietly insults: your body runs this system better than any counting app could.

Dissolved in your blood is a certain concentration of salts and other particles — its *osmolality*, normally held in a narrow band around 280 to 295 milliosmoles per kilogram. When you lose water, that concentration rises. A change of only about one to two percent is enough to trip two responses at once: your brain releases a hormone (ADH) that tells your kidneys to hold onto water and concentrate your urine, and it makes you **thirsty**.

Thirst isn't a vague nag. It's a sensor reporting a regulated variable, the way a thermostat reads temperature. You don't count breaths to avoid suffocating. You don't need to count glasses to avoid drying out. Drink when you're thirsty and the system holds your osmolality in band without you doing any arithmetic.

## The real danger runs the other way

The rule teaches a one-sided fear: never enough. The documented harm points the opposite direction.

In 2005 the *New England Journal of Medicine* published blood tests from 488 runners at the Boston Marathon. Thirteen percent finished with hyponatremia — sodium diluted to a dangerous low — and 0.6 percent were in the critical range. The strongest predictors weren't dehydration. They were drinking more than three litres during the race and *gaining* weight while running: too much water, not too little.

It can be fatal. In January 2007 a 28-year-old woman named Jennifer Strange drank close to two gallons of water in a few hours during a radio contest — "Hold Your Wee for a Wii" — and died of water intoxication that afternoon. Her blood was diluted until her brain swelled.

None of this means water is bad, and honesty cuts both ways. A 2008 review in the *Journal of the American Society of Nephrology* ("Just Add Water") went hunting for the promised benefits of extra water — clearer skin, weight loss, better organ function — and found no clear evidence for any of them. The same authors were careful to add that the evidence doesn't rule benefit *out*, either, and that some conditions are real exceptions: people prone to recurrent kidney stones, for instance, genuinely do better drinking more. Heat, hard exercise, illness and certain medical conditions change the math too — and thirst is a weaker signal in older adults, who can quietly under-drink if they wait to feel it. The point isn't a new quota. It's that the old one was invented.

## What to actually take from this

The useful thing here isn't "eight is wrong, drink seven." It's watching *how* a real recommendation mutated — because the same edit happens to health advice constantly. Three questions catch most of it:

1. **All sources, or just one?** The 1945 figure counted every drop, including the water in food. The myth counts only the glass in your hand.
2. **A population average, or a personal mandate?** "About 2.5 litres in most instances" became "you, specifically, must hit eight."
3. **What did the next sentence say?** The caveat that undid the whole thing was one clause away — and it's exactly the part that got cut.

The 8×8 rule fails all three. Most confident, round-number health rules fail at least one. So drink when you're thirsty — but when the next such rule turns up, don't reach for a glass to prove your virtue. Reach for the sentence that got deleted.
