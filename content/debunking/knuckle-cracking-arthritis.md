For sixty years, Dr. Donald Unger cracked the knuckles of his left hand at least twice a day. He never cracked his right. He did this on purpose, the way you'd run an experiment, because as a boy he'd been told — by his mother, two aunts, and a mother-in-law later on — that cracking his knuckles would give him arthritis.

By his own count the left hand got cracked at least 36,500 times. The right hand, none. At the end he X-rayed both. No arthritis in either. No difference between them. He published the result in 1998 in *Arthritis & Rheumatism* under the title "Does Knuckle Cracking Lead to Arthritis of the Fingers?" and in 2009 he won an Ig Nobel Prize for it. His conclusion, addressed to the four women who'd warned him, was that there was "no apparent consequence."

It's a wonderful story. It is also, as evidence, almost worthless — and the gap between those two facts is the whole point.

## Why one stubborn doctor doesn't settle anything

Unger is a sample size of one. Maybe his joints were unusually tough. Maybe sixty years of cracking *does* cause arthritis in most people and he got lucky. A single self-experiment can't tell the difference between "this is safe" and "this was safe for me." It's a great anecdote and a terrible study, and Unger, a physician, surely knew that. The charm is the point: he made an unfalsifiable family superstition feel testable.

The thing that actually settles the question is far more boring. In 2011, deWeber and colleagues ran a case-control study of 215 people aged 50 to 89 — some with hand osteoarthritis confirmed on X-ray, some without — and asked about their knuckle-cracking history. There was no association. Not at any frequency, not at any duration. Published in the *Journal of the American Board of Family Medicine* (2011;24[2]:169–174). If anything the crackers trended toward *slightly less* arthritis — 18% versus 22%, almost certainly noise, but pointedly not the direction the myth predicts.

So the honest answer isn't "one doctor proved it." It's "the population data show no link, and the one guy who cracked half a million times is consistent with that." The second sentence is less fun and more true.

## Why the myth won't die

Here's the part worth keeping. The belief survives because of the *sound*. A knuckle crack is loud, sudden, and feels structural — like something gave way inside the joint. Intuition reads that crunch as damage. It isn't: a 2015 real-time MRI study watched it happen, and the pop is a gas bubble *forming* in the joint fluid, not cartilage grinding. But the noise arrives first, and the X-ray showing nothing arrives never, because you don't X-ray your own hands.

*The crunch is a bubble appearing, not a joint wearing out.*

This is the general shape of a sticky health myth: a vivid sensory signal stands in for evidence you'll never collect. The cracking feels harmful, so the claim that it's harmful feels confirmed every single time you do it. Decades of population studies can't compete with a sound you hear with your own joints.

The transferable move is to notice when your evidence *is* a sensation. "It feels like it must be bad for me" is a hypothesis, not a finding — and it's exactly the kind of hypothesis a sound, a twinge, or a queasy feeling is very good at making you stop testing. Unger's gift wasn't the X-ray. It was sixty years of refusing to let the crunch be the last word.

Crack away. Annoying to the people around you, yes. Bad for your joints, no — and we know that from 215 strangers, not one stubborn doctor.
