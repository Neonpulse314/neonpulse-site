You've probably heard this one, and it's a good story. Go to an old cathedral, find a window that's been in place for six or seven hundred years, and look closely at the panes. Many of them are visibly thicker at the bottom than at the top. The explanation people love: glass isn't really a solid. It's a liquid — an extremely slow one — and over those centuries it has been quietly flowing downward, pooling at the base of each pane like honey left on a counter for a very long time.

The thick bottoms are real. The conclusion is not. And the actual reason is a better story than the myth.

## The observation is true, the cause isn't

It's correct that a lot of medieval window glass is uneven, often thicker at one edge. But "flow" makes a prediction, and the windows don't keep it.

Before the float-glass process was invented in the 1950s, window panes were made by hand, and the common methods produced sheets of uneven thickness. In the **crown glass** method, a glassblower spun a blob of molten glass into a flat disc using centrifugal force; the disc came out thicker toward the centre and thinner at the rim. **Cylinder glass** — blowing a long bubble, cutting it open, and flattening it — left ripples and varying thickness too. Either way, you ended up with panes that had a thick side and a thin side.

A glazier cutting these into window-sized rectangles had a choice about which way was up. Setting the heavier edge at the bottom is the sensible, stable thing to do, so that's usually what they did. The thick bottoms aren't a record of eight centuries of flow. They're a record of how a craftsman set the glass on the day it went in.

And here's the detail that kills the flow theory outright: not all of them are thick at the bottom. Survey enough old windows and you find panes thicker at the **top**, or on a **side**. If gravity were slowly pulling the glass down, that simply couldn't happen. The unevenness points to manufacturing and installation, not to a liquid creeping toward the floor.

## So what is glass, then?

Glass is an **amorphous solid.** That phrase is doing real work, so it's worth unpacking.

When most liquids cool and solidify, their molecules lock into a neat, repeating crystal lattice — that's what happens when water becomes ice. Glass doesn't do that. Cool it fast enough and the molecules freeze in place while still arranged in the jumbled, disordered way they were as a liquid. So the *arrangement* is liquid-like — no long-range order — but the *behaviour* is solid: it holds its shape, it shatters rather than pours, it bears load.

That disordered structure is the kernel of truth the myth feeds on. "The molecules are arranged like a liquid" is true, and it's a short, lazy step from there to "so it must still be a liquid." But how a material is arranged and how it mechanically behaves are different questions. At room temperature, glass behaves like a solid. Full stop.

## The number that ends the argument

You can settle this with viscosity — the measure of how a material resists flowing. Glass *does* technically flow, the way technically everything does. The question is how fast, and the answer is the punchline.

The materials scientist Edgar Dutra Zanotto worked it out in a 1998 paper with the wonderfully direct title *"Do cathedral glasses flow?"* Using the actual viscosity of medieval-type glass at ordinary temperatures, he estimated the time required for any flow to become **visible** at room temperature. The answer is a number with more than twenty zeros after it — vastly, comically longer than the current age of the universe, which is a mere 1.4 × 10¹⁰ years (13.8 billion).

In other words: those cathedral windows have not flowed a measurable amount, and they won't in any timeframe that means anything to a human, a civilisation, or a planet. To get glass to actually flow, you have to heat it until it softens — which is, not coincidentally, exactly what you do to shape it in the first place.

## Why this myth is so sticky

It survives because it has everything a good myth needs: a visible piece of "evidence" you can go and see for yourself, a counterintuitive twist ("the windows are secretly liquid!"), and a grain of real science underneath ("amorphous structure") that makes it sound informed rather than made up. It even gets repeated in classrooms, which launders it into something that feels authoritative.

But the windows are telling you about a glassblower's afternoon, not about the slow ooze of time. The glass set on the day it was installed and it has been a solid every day since. The far more impressive fact is the one the myth talks you out of noticing: someone, eight hundred years ago, spun molten sand into a sheet thin enough to see through, and it is still there, still clear, still holding its shape — precisely because it never flowed at all.
