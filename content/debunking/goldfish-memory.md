You've heard it your whole life. A goldfish has a three-second memory. Every lap of the bowl is a fresh world; it can't be bored because it can't remember being anywhere.

It's false. More useful is *how* you could have caught it without knowing a single thing about fish.

## Start with the number, not the fish

Ask people how long a goldfish remembers and you get three seconds. Then you get five. Then seven. Some tellings just say "a few seconds." The figure moves depending on who's talking.

That drift is the whole story.

A measured quantity doesn't wander like that. If someone had actually run the experiment, the claim would have arrived attached to its measurement — a species, a number of animals, a task, a retention interval, a journal, a year. Those details travel *with* real findings, because the finding is inseparable from how it was obtained.

The goldfish claim arrives with none of them. No study, no author, no date. Just a number that changes in transit — which is exactly what a number does when there's nothing anchoring it. It isn't a finding that turned out to be wrong. It's a piece of folklore wearing a unit.

So before checking whether goldfish remember: notice that nobody ever said who checked.

## What goldfish actually do

Now the fish.

In a study published in 2022, researchers at Ben-Gurion University put goldfish in a water tank mounted on a wheeled, motorised chassis. A camera tracked the fish's position and orientation inside the tank, and a computer translated its swimming into movement of the vehicle across the floor of a room. Swim toward that wall, the cart rolls that way.

Six goldfish were trained to drive this thing to a target on the far side of the room for a food reward. They learned it. They reached the target from different starting positions, corrected their course, and worked around dead ends rather than grinding into them.

Set aside how funny that is and look at what it requires. To steer toward a goal you can't reach yet, you must hold the goal in mind while you move, treat the room beyond the glass as a stable place, register that your own motion changes where you stand in it, and carry all of that forward across minutes. The training ran over days.

None of that is available to an animal that forgets everything every three seconds. The result doesn't merely outperform the myth — it's incompatible with it.

And fish memory generally runs far longer than the folklore allows. Culum Brown trained crimson spotted rainbowfish to escape a model trawl net through a hole in it. Retested eleven months later, having not seen the apparatus since, they still escaped promptly. Eleven months is a substantial fraction of that animal's life. (Rainbowfish, not goldfish — a different species, so treat it as evidence about fish memory in general, not a goldfish number. It's still eleven months from a single training episode in an animal supposedly incapable of holding anything.)

## Why this particular myth stuck

Most myths are just errors. This one does a job.

If a goldfish forgets its world every few seconds, then a bare bowl costs it nothing. No enrichment needed, no space needed, nothing to feel bad about — it can't be bored, because boredom requires remembering that you were already here. The three-second memory is what makes the bowl acceptable.

Myths that license something we already do are stickier than myths that don't, because correcting them has a price. This one asks you to reconsider the container.

## The tool you can take with you

The transferable part isn't about fish.

**When a claim arrives as a bare number, ask what the number is attached to.** Not "is it true" — that usually needs expertise you don't have. Ask the cheaper question: does this figure come with a method? A real measurement has a shape behind it — someone did something specific, to something specific, and wrote down what happened. You can go and look.

Then apply the drift test. Watch the same claim in three or four places. A measured figure stays put, because it's tethered; the retellings converge on it. An unmeasured figure wanders, because each teller is reaching for something that sounds right rather than something they read. Three seconds, five, seven — that spread isn't a rounding disagreement. It's the sound of a claim with nothing underneath it.

You can run this on a lot more than goldfish. "Humans use 10% of their brains." "You swallow eight spiders a year." "You need eight glasses of water a day." Don't start by arguing. Start by asking which study, on whom, measuring what — and watch whether the number holds still when you look at it from a second angle.

The goldfish is a good place to practise, because here the answer is unusually clean: nobody who repeats the figure can point to where it came from, and the fish can drive.
