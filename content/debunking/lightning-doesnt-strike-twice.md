The Empire State Building gets struck by lightning about 20 to 25 times per year. In a single storm, it can be hit multiple times within minutes. The building has its own lightning rod system specifically because repeated strikes are the expected reality, not the exception.

"Lightning never strikes the same place twice" is not just wrong. It describes roughly the opposite of how lightning works.

## How Lightning Actually Chooses Where to Strike

Lightning doesn't choose randomly. It follows a path of least electrical resistance between a charged cloud and the ground.

When a thunderstorm builds up, charge separates within the cloud — negative charge accumulates at the bottom, inducing a positive charge on the ground below. When the potential difference becomes large enough, the air breaks down and current flows.

The path that forms isn't random — it's shaped by whatever provides the easiest route for electrons to travel. Tall objects reduce the distance between cloud and ground, which reduces the resistance of that path. Pointed objects concentrate the electric field, making them even more likely to initiate or attract a strike.

A tall building, a lone tree on a hilltop, a radio tower — these are repeatedly preferred routes for the same reason every time. The physics doesn't change between storms. If a location was a good conductor path yesterday, it's still a good conductor path today.

## Where the Myth Came From

The phrase appears to have originated as a folk saying well before anyone understood lightning mechanics. In a world without physics education, the idea had a certain intuitive appeal — lightning felt random and chaotic, so a place that had been hit might seem "used up" or somehow excluded from future hits.

Benjamin Franklin's work in the 1750s — including the famous kite experiment and the invention of the lightning rod — should have ended the myth. His entire insight was that lightning was electricity and could be reliably directed to safe paths through conductors. Lightning rods work precisely because lightning will predictably strike the highest nearby conductor repeatedly. You don't build a lightning rod by hoping the roof won't be hit again.

But the folk saying survived. Sayings often outlast the corrections.

## Structures That Prove the Point

The Empire State Building is the most cited example, but it's not unique:

- The CN Tower in Toronto is struck roughly 75 times per year and has a dedicated lightning protection system that records each strike.
- Radio and cell towers in open terrain are struck so frequently that their grounding systems are designed as primary infrastructure, not as a precaution.
- The Eiffel Tower was struck 4 times in a single storm in 1902, early in its life, and has been struck thousands of times since.

These aren't unusual events. They're the designed purpose of lightning rods on tall structures: accept the strike, conduct it safely to ground, repeat as needed.

## What "Don't Stand Under a Tree" Actually Means

The practical safety advice associated with lightning — avoid tall isolated objects, don't stand under trees, stay low in open fields — is correct, but it's correct because those things *attract* lightning, not because they've already been hit and are now safe.

A tree that's been struck is still the tallest conductor in the area. It's more likely to be struck again, not less. If anything, a strike may have compromised its structure, making it a more dangerous place to stand near — but not because lightning is avoiding it.

The correct inference from "that tree was just struck" is "get away from that tree." Not "we're safe now."
