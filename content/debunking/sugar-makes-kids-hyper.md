Ask almost any parent and they'll tell you the same thing: let a kid loose on birthday cake and soda, and twenty minutes later they're vibrating. It feels less like a belief than an observation — they've *seen* it, over and over.

They have seen something. It just isn't what they think. When researchers test sugar under controlled conditions, the effect on children's behavior keeps coming up empty. The hyperactivity is real — but the evidence says it lives in the person watching, not in the child's blood.

## The experiment that gives it away

In 1994, psychologists Daniel Hoover and Richard Milich ran a study that should be more famous than it is. They recruited 35 boys, aged five to seven, whose mothers described them as behaviorally "sugar-sensitive" — the kids who supposedly can't handle it.

Every child was given the same drink: a placebo, sweetened with aspartame. No sugar at all. Then the mothers were split into two groups. Half were told their son had just had a large dose of sugar. The other half were told he'd had the placebo.

Then mother and son were filmed playing together, and each mother was asked how her child behaved.

The mothers who believed their sons had eaten sugar rated them as **significantly more hyperactive** — even though every boy had the identical sugar-free drink. And it wasn't only their ratings. On the tape, those mothers kept their sons physically closer and under tighter control — and trended toward criticizing, watching, and talking at them more. The belief didn't just bias what they saw; it changed what they *did*.

The sugar was never the variable. The expectation was.

## When kids actually eat the sugar

Maybe the placebo trick is too clever — what happens when children really do load up on sugar? That was tested directly, and the answer is the same: nothing.

Also in 1994, a team led by Mark Wolraich published a double-blind trial in the *New England Journal of Medicine*. Two groups of children — 25 normal preschoolers and 23 school-age kids their own parents had labeled sugar-sensitive — spent three consecutive three-week stretches on different diets. One was high in sucrose, above what most kids normally eat. The others used aspartame or saccharin instead. Nobody — not the families, not the researchers — knew which diet was in play at any time.

A few scattered differences turned up — about what you'd expect from chance across dozens of measures, with no pattern to them. And among the older children their own parents had singled out as sugar-sensitive, there was nothing at all: no difference on any of the thirty-nine things the researchers tracked, even when those kids were genuinely eating extra sugar.

The next year, Wolraich and colleagues pooled the double-blind studies into a meta-analysis in *JAMA*. No measurable effect turned up — every estimate was small and statistically indistinguishable from zero. The authors were careful about what that does and doesn't prove: a small effect, or an effect in some subset of children, can't be *ruled out* — but there was no evidence for the big, obvious switch parents describe. And they named the likelier source of the belief: expectancy, and simple association. Sugar shows up at birthday parties, holidays, and late nights out — exactly the loud, crowded, overtired situations where any kid goes wild. Cake didn't cause the chaos. Cake just happened to be there for it. That was 1994 and 1995, and nothing in the three decades since has overturned it.

## The tool worth keeping

Forget the sugar for a second, because the useful part isn't the verdict — it's the mechanism. What Hoover and Milich isolated is the **expectancy effect**: when you believe a cause is present, you don't neutrally observe its supposed effect. You look for it, you weight ambiguous evidence toward it, you remember the hits and forget the misses — and, as the hovering mothers showed, you can nudge the situation into producing it.

This is why a sugar pill can relieve real pain, why the emergency room "feels busier" on a full moon that the records show is an ordinary night, why a coach's "problem player" keeps getting benched for fouls the favored player commits freely. Any time someone says "X makes people Y," a specific question cuts through it: *would they still see Y if they didn't know whether X had happened?* If the effect evaporates when the observer is blinded, that's strong evidence it was never in X — it was in the expectation.

That's also the whole reason the good studies gave everyone a placebo and hid the conditions. A double-blind design isn't bureaucratic caution; it's the only way to separate what a substance does from what we expect it to do. Once you've seen how far apart those two things can drift, you start noticing how often everyday "obvious" causes have never actually been blinded at all.

None of this means sugar is harmless — it does plenty to teeth and long-term health. It just doesn't flip a hyperactivity switch. The quiet cost of believing it does is that the real reasons a kid is bouncing — the party, the crowd, the missed nap, the sheer excitement — get waved off, while the frosting takes the blame it was never owed.
