When Marcus told his history class that Napoleon was actually average height, his teacher looked skeptical.

"He was famously short," she said. "That's why he had a Napoleon complex."

"The thing is," Marcus said, "I think that's based on a measurement error."

She let him look it up.

He was right.

---

Napoleon Bonaparte was recorded at 5 feet 2 inches in contemporary documents. That sounds short. The problem is that the measurement was taken in French inches — and French inches were longer than English inches.

Converted to the modern metric system, Napoleon stood approximately 168–170 centimetres. That's roughly 5 feet 6 or 7 inches.

The average height of a French man in the early 19th century was about 165 centimetres. Napoleon was above average for his time and place.

The Duke of Wellington — the British general who defeated Napoleon at Waterloo and became one of his most famous critics — stood about 5'9". That's taller than Napoleon, but not by a dramatic margin. The two men were not the extreme contrast the caricatures suggest.

---

So where did "short Napoleon" come from?

Two places: British propaganda and a mistranslation that stuck.

**The propaganda**: During the Napoleonic Wars, Britain and France were enemies for most of two decades. British satirists — particularly the cartoonist James Gillray — drew Napoleon as a tiny, petulant figure being managed by larger, more reasonable men. Gillray's caricatures were widely circulated and enormously influential. They weren't reporting fact; they were wartime mockery. But the image lodged.

**The unit confusion**: When Napoleon's height was recorded in French documents and later read by English speakers, the number was taken as English inches rather than French inches. The same number — 5'2" — meant different things in the two systems. No one noticed the conversion was missing. The error circulated in enough places that it became "common knowledge."

Napoleon's own soldiers called him *le petit caporal* — the little corporal. But this was an affectionate nickname, not a description of his height. It referred to his early days serving alongside soldiers, his casual manner with troops, his willingness to eat and sleep in the field. "Little" in French can be affectionate the way "old boy" is in English. It was a term of familiarity, not mockery.

---

It's worth asking why this particular myth is so persistent.

Part of it is that it's useful. "Short man with a big ego overcompensating through conquest" is a psychologically satisfying story. It explains a complex historical figure with a simple motive. Napoleon as tragic short man is more entertaining than Napoleon as a genuinely talented military strategist who rose from minor Corsican nobility to reshape Europe.

The myth also has the structure of a put-down, which makes it easy to remember and repeat. It gives people something to say about Napoleon without having to know much about him.

The clinical term "Napoleon complex" — the idea that short men compensate for their height through aggression and dominance — was named after this myth, not after documented patterns in short men. The research on whether short stature actually predicts aggressive behaviour is, at best, mixed. The name stuck because the myth was already in place.

---

Napoleon was not a simple man. He reformed the French legal system (the Napoleonic Code is still the basis of law in France, Quebec, and Louisiana, among other places). He reorganized European borders in ways that lasted generations. He was defeated, exiled, escaped, raised another army, was defeated again, and died in exile on a remote island at 51.

He was not short. He was, by the standards of his time and country, average to slightly above average.

The story of the short Napoleon is a story about how propaganda, translation errors, and the human love of a satisfying explanation can turn a fiction into a fact — and then name a psychological disorder after it.

---

**The real story in brief:**
- Napoleon's height in French documents: ~5'2" in French inches
- French inch ≠ English inch — converted, he was ~5'7" or 168–170 cm
- Average French male height ca. 1800: ~165 cm — Napoleon was above average
- "Le petit caporal" was an affectionate nickname, not a height comment
- The short image originated in British wartime caricatures, especially James Gillray
- "Napoleon complex" was named after the myth, not documented behaviour
