Every winter someone repeats it: put a hat on, you lose most of your body heat through your head. The number attached to it is usually somewhere between forty and eighty percent. It has the feel of settled fact — the kind of thing a parent says and a school nurse confirms.

It traces to a real document. A 1970s US Army survival manual told soldiers that 40 to 45 percent of body heat is lost through the head, and advised keeping it covered in the cold. The manual wasn't inventing the figure. It rests on a military experiment from the 1950s, in which volunteers were dressed in Arctic survival suits and exposed to brutal cold while researchers measured how fast they lost heat.

Here is the part that matters. The suits covered the volunteers from the neck down. The head was the only thing left bare. So of course almost all the measured heat loss came from the head — because the head was the only part of them that *could* lose heat freely. Insulate everything except one patch of skin, and that patch becomes your main heat leak. It would have been just as true of a bare forearm.

The experiment measured what happens when the head is the only exposed part. Somewhere between the lab and the field manual, that got rewritten into a claim about the head *itself* — as though the skull were a special radiator, shedding heat at a rate the rest of the body doesn't.

It isn't. In 2008, in the *British Medical Journal*, Rachel Vreeman and Aaron Carroll worked through the claim as part of a paper on medical myths. The head accounts for roughly 7 to 10 percent of the body's surface area. A bare head in the cold loses heat in proportion to that surface — call it around a tenth of the total, not forty percent, not eighty. There is nothing about the head that makes it leak heat faster per square inch than a bare chest or a bare leg of the same size. When researchers have actually measured it, an uncovered head loses heat about in line with its share of the body's surface, and no more.

So the number is wrong. But the honest version of this isn't "your head doesn't matter, skip the hat." Two things keep the advice good even though the reasoning behind it was false.

First: the head is usually the part you leave uncovered. You bundle your torso, your legs, your hands — and then walk out with a bare head and face. Under those conditions you really do lose a large share of your heat up top, for the same boring reason the volunteers did: it's the exposed part. Cover it and you close the biggest open door.

Second: the head and face carry a rich blood supply close to the surface and less insulating fat than much of the body, so per unit of skin they can give up heat a little faster than, say, a well-padded midsection. A little. Not four times faster.

So wear the hat. Just don't believe the reason you were handed. The head isn't special; the arithmetic is. Cover whatever you leave bare, because heat leaves through whatever you leave bare — and in most winters that's your head, because you forgot to cover it, not because it's a chimney.

The myth is a good small lesson in how a fact goes wrong. Nobody lied. There was a real experiment, run competently, that produced a real number. And the number was true — for the exact conditions of the test, where the head was the only thing uncovered. The error was carrying that number out of those conditions and treating it as a property of the head. That is one of the most common ways a true measurement turns into a false belief: strip away the setup that made it true, keep the figure, and pass it along.

Check the setup before you trust the number. Ask what was covered, and what was left bare.
