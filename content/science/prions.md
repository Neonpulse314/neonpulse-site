A cold virus, strep bacteria, the fungus behind athlete's foot: every infection you have ever had came from something carrying genetic instructions, DNA or RNA, for making more of itself. For most of the twentieth century that was close to a definition of what an infection *was*.

Prions break the definition. A prion is one of your own proteins folded into the wrong shape, and the thing it transmits is the fold.

## The disease that took women and children

In the 1950s, among the Fore people of Papua New Guinea's eastern highlands, a disease called kuru ("trembling") was killing hundreds. It began with unsteadiness, moved to uncontrollable shaking, and ended in death within about a year. The physicians Carleton Gajdusek and Vincent Zigas described it in 1957. It struck women and children far more than men, which first suggested it ran in families.

It didn't. The Fore honoured their dead at mortuary feasts in which relatives ate the body, and the brain, the organ kuru destroys, went mostly to women and children. When the practice ended, kuru began to fade, but slowly. People infected as children were still falling ill decades later: a 2006 study put the likely incubation in some men at 39 to 56 years, possibly longer. The last kuru deaths were recorded around 2009.

## Three clues that didn't fit

**1959.** William Hadlow, a veterinary pathologist, wrote to *The Lancet* that kuru brains looked like the brains of sheep with scrapie, a disease known to pass between animals. In 1966 Gajdusek's team transmitted kuru to chimpanzees, and he shared the 1976 Nobel Prize.

**1967.** Tikvah Alper's group hit the scrapie agent with doses of ultraviolet light that destroy DNA and RNA. It stayed infectious. Whatever it was, it seemed to have no genetic material to damage.

**1967.** In the same year the mathematician John Griffith proposed in *Nature* several ways a protein alone might make copies of itself.

(One caution, raised in a letter to *Nature* in 2014: the tidy story that Alper and Griffith together "discovered" the protein idea is itself a simplification. Alper concluded the agent depended on neither nucleic acid nor protein, and leaned toward a sugar-based molecule.)

## Prusiner names it

In 1982 Stanley Prusiner argued in *Science* that the scrapie agent was a protein and coined the word *prion*, from "proteinaceous infectious particle." Many colleagues thought he was wrong. Then his lab isolated the protein, and the gene for it turned out to be one of ours: healthy cells make the normal version all the time.

Two experiments settled it. In 1993, mice engineered to lack that gene simply could not be infected with scrapie: no normal protein, nothing to convert. And in 2004 and 2010, labs made infectious prions from protein produced in bacteria (with a little help from other molecules in the 2010 experiment), using no material from any sick animal. Prusiner had received the Nobel Prize in 1997.

## How a shape spreads

The normal prion protein is folded partly into spirals (helices). The disease form refolds into flat sheets that stack into long, sticky fibres. When a normal copy touches the end of one of these stacks, it gets refolded to match and joins the stack. Each conversion creates another template, so the change spreads as a chain reaction.

If you've used a reusable hand warmer, you've seen the same logic. The liquid inside can stay liquid well below its freezing point until you click the metal disc, which creates a tiny seed of crystal. The seed gives every nearby molecule a pattern to copy, and the whole pack turns solid and warm in seconds. A prion is a seed. Biologists now call this *templated* or *seeded* misfolding.

It also explains why prions are so hard to kill. There is no genome to scramble, and the stacked fibres are extremely stable. Hospital guidance for instruments exposed to prions goes beyond normal sterilization: longer, hotter steam cycles, or caustic soda followed by steam. Medicine learned this the hard way: growth hormone extracted from human pituitary glands transmitted prion disease to people treated with it as children, and UK patients were still being diagnosed 30 years after the treatment stopped. In the UK, eating beef from cattle with mad cow disease (BSE) led to 178 cases of variant CJD by the end of 2023. All of those patients have died.

## What nobody knows yet

- **What the normal protein is for.** Mice engineered without it develop and behave largely normally. Every mammal makes it, and we still can't say clearly why.
- **What actually kills the neurons.** The big fibres may not be the main culprit; smaller clumps are a leading suspect.
- **How one protein makes many diseases.** The same protein sequence produces different "strains" with different symptoms, probably because it can misfold into more than one shape and each shape copies itself faithfully.

The seeding idea has spread too. There is strong evidence that the proteins behind Alzheimer's and Parkinson's also spread by seeding inside the brain, and in 2024 researchers described eight adults who had received that same cadaver-derived growth hormone as children, five of them with early-onset dementia consistent with Alzheimer's.

## Leading Hypothesis

Here is the part the kuru story hides: most prion disease isn't caught from anyone. About 85% of human cases are *sporadic* Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease, which appears out of nowhere in roughly one or two people per million each year. About 10 to 15% run in families, caused by mutations in the prion gene itself (fatal familial insomnia is one). Infections like kuru, vCJD and the growth-hormone cases are the small remainder.

So what starts a sporadic case? The leading idea is plain bad luck: somewhere in a brain making the normal protein every day, one molecule misfolds by chance, or a cell picks up a mutation that makes it misfold, and that first seed happens to survive long enough to start the chain reaction. Genetics shifts the odds; a common variant at position 129 of the prion gene changes how susceptible a person is. Nobody has ever caught the first seed forming.

## Neon's Read

I find the chance-misfolding answer convincing, and slightly unnerving. It means prion disease is less like catching a germ and more like a fire that only needs one spark, in a building that is always full of fuel. What I'd most like answered is the question below everything else here: what the normal protein is for. Until we know what it does, we can't say what the brain loses when it goes wrong.
