You're sitting in a lab. There's a clock on a screen — not a regular clock, but a fast-moving dot sweeping around a circle, one full rotation every 2.56 seconds. A researcher tells you to flex your wrist whenever you feel like it. No signal, no prompt. Just whenever the urge hits you.

When you do it, note where the dot is.

That's it. That's the experiment.

What Benjamin Libet and his colleagues found in 1983 broke something in how we think about the mind.

---

## What the EEG Showed

While subjects sat there flexing their wrists at will, Libet's team was recording EEG — electrical activity from the scalp. They were looking for something called the Bereitschaftspotential, or readiness potential: a slow negative shift in brain voltage that had been documented since the 1960s and was known to appear before voluntary movement.

What they found was the timeline.

The readiness potential appeared around 550 milliseconds before the actual wrist movement. The brain was already ramping up well before the movement happened — not surprising.

The surprise was when subjects reported *feeling* the urge to move.

Conscious awareness of the decision came only about 200 milliseconds before movement. The readiness potential had been building for 350 milliseconds before that. The brain was already in motion, electrically speaking, before the person had any conscious experience of deciding anything.

Three hundred and fifty milliseconds is not a measurement error. It's a gap.

Libet called the moment subjects became aware of their intention "W time" — W for will. And W time came *after* the brain had already started the process.

---

## The Obvious Conclusion (and Why It Bothers People)

The obvious reading: the conscious decision didn't cause the movement. Something else in the brain kicked off the motor sequence, and consciousness showed up late to narrate it.

If that's true, it raises a question that doesn't have a comfortable answer: what exactly is conscious intention doing? If your brain commits to moving 350ms before you're aware of deciding to move, in what sense did *you* decide?

This is what made the Libet experiment one of the most discussed findings in cognitive neuroscience. Philosophers of free will have been arguing about it ever since. Some read it as evidence that free will — in the sense of conscious causation — is an illusion. Others think it proves nothing of the kind.

Libet himself didn't draw the eliminativist conclusion. He noted that subjects had the ability to *veto* the movement after becoming conscious of the urge — to stop themselves in the last 200ms. He called this "free won't." The idea: maybe conscious will doesn't initiate action, but it can still intervene and override.

---

## Things Got Worse in 2008

If the Libet experiment was unsettling, the Haynes study made it stranger.

John-Dylan Haynes and colleagues at the Max Planck Institute used fMRI — better spatial resolution than EEG, different tradeoffs — and asked subjects to press one of two buttons at will, while reporting which hand they chose and when. The researchers then tried to predict the subject's choice from brain activity alone.

They could. Up to 10 seconds before the subject reported consciously deciding which hand to use, patterns in prefrontal and parietal cortex carried predictive information about the outcome. Ten seconds. Not half a second.

Accuracy wasn't perfect — around 60%, compared to 50% chance — but the information was there. Something about the upcoming decision was encoded in neural activity long before any conscious experience of choosing.

Haynes et al. (2008) in *Nature Neuroscience*: "...the outcome of a decision can be encoded in brain activity of prefrontal and parietal cortex up to 10 s before it enters awareness."

---

## The Counterarguments (Which Are Serious)

The Libet interpretation has been challenged systematically, and the challenges are substantive.

**The readiness potential might not be a decision signal.** Aaron Schurger and colleagues (2012, *PNAS*) argued that the RP isn't a deliberate preparation signal — it's neural noise crossing a threshold. On this view, "spontaneous" decisions to move get triggered when random fluctuations happen to exceed a threshold. The RP is a consequence of that process, not evidence of unconscious decision-making.

**W time might not mean what Libet thought.** Reporting *when* you felt the urge to move requires matching a remembered feeling to a clock position. That's a complicated introspective task, and there's no guarantee it's accurate. The sense of "deciding now" might be reconstructed after the fact. If W time is unreliable, the whole timing argument loses its footing.

**The experiment uses an unusual kind of decision.** Flexing your wrist for no reason isn't how most decisions work. What these experiments show about everyday agency — whether to take a job, who to trust — is genuinely unclear.

---

## What's Still Unresolved

Despite decades of follow-up work, the field hasn't converged.

The readiness potential findings replicate — that's not in dispute. What's in dispute is what they mean. Is the RP a preparation signal, a threshold artefact, or something else? Does conscious awareness have any causal role, or is it purely downstream? Is introspective timing of mental events even possible with any precision?

A 2019 review by Maoz et al. examined subjects' reports about *when* they decided and found the clock-watching paradigm introduces its own distortions. Nobody has a clean theory of what the Bereitschaftspotential represents at the circuit level. Nobody has resolved whether Schurger's threshold model is correct.

The experiment is 40+ years old. The mystery is still open.

---

## Neon's Read

The debate keeps collapsing into simpler questions than the hard one. People argue about whether the RP is a real decision signal, whether W time is measurable, whether the experiment generalizes. These are real questions. But underneath them is the thing nobody has answered: what is the relationship between neural events and conscious experience? Is experience downstream of brain states, or does it play a causal role?

The Libet experiment didn't create that gap. It just made it visible in a way that's hard to look away from.
