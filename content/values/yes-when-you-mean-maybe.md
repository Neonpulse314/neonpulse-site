Tyler's friend Priya asked him to co-lead their group presentation. She asked specifically because he was good at public speaking, and the group had a graded slot they couldn't afford to waste. Tyler was stretched thin — three other assignments, a part-time job shift that week, a general dread of adding anything to the list. But Priya was standing right there, looking hopeful.

He said yes.

He meant: *probably yes, if I can figure out the scheduling. I'll deal with it later.*

Priya heard: *yes.*

She restructured the presentation around two speakers. Wrote Tyler's section. Told the other group members, who stopped worrying about coverage. Told the professor, who logged it. She spent an evening mapping out their back-and-forth so it would flow cleanly.

Three days before the presentation, Tyler said he couldn't do it. He was too stressed. He was sorry. He'd thought he could handle it.

Priya had seventy-two hours. She rewrote the whole thing solo. Presentation day: she looked tired. Their grade came back lower than it should have.

Tyler felt bad. He also, somewhere underneath it, didn't feel fully responsible. He had never explicitly promised. He'd *thought* he could do it when he said yes. Things just got complicated.

---

Here is what actually happened.

When Tyler said yes, Priya received a clean signal. She didn't receive "Tyler is currently at 60% confidence he can do this." She received *yes*, which in the planning context meant: *he's in, build around him.*

The ambiguity Tyler held internally was invisible to Priya. Everything she built — the structure, the timing, her own reduced anxiety — was built on the external signal, not his internal state. When the internal state collapsed, the external structure went with it. But by then, she'd spent real time on something that wouldn't work.

This is the asymmetry of a soft yes: the uncertainty stays with the person who gave it, but the risk transfers to the person who received it.

---

There's research on why this happens so consistently. Mark Leary's sociometer theory describes how humans monitor social acceptance in real time, almost continuously. When someone makes a direct request and you hesitate, you feel the strain of possible rejection immediately — like a meter dropping. Saying yes restores it. The feeling of resolving the discomfort is immediate and real.

The consequences of that yes — Priya's seventy-two-hour scramble, the grade — are abstract and distant at the moment of speaking. They don't register as costs yet. So the calculation runs like this: present discomfort (the awkward "I'm not sure") versus a future consequence (their problem, not yet real). The present wins almost every time.

Roy Baumeister's research on interpersonal conflict adds another layer. When he asked people to describe conflicts from both perspectives — as the person who caused harm and as the person harmed — the accounts were systematically different. Perpetrators minimized consequences, emphasized their good intentions, and focused on the complexity of their situation. Victims focused on the harm itself. Tyler's version: *I got overwhelmed, I was going to try, it just didn't work out.* Priya's experience: three days of extra work and a worse grade.

Both accounts are probably sincere. But only one of them matches what actually happened to the person who planned around the yes.

---

The thirty-second conversation Tyler avoided — *"I want to help but I'm genuinely not sure I can commit to this right now"* — would have been uncomfortable. Priya might have been disappointed. Tyler would have felt that meter drop.

But Priya would have had the actual information. She could have found someone else, restructured the presentation, adjusted her plan. She had the time. There would have been no seventy-two-hour scramble because she would have known from the start that this piece wasn't locked in.

The real cost of the soft yes isn't that it's dishonest. Tyler didn't lie to himself or to Priya — he genuinely intended to follow through. The cost is structural: it moves the uncertainty out of the planning phase, where it's useful and manageable, and into the execution phase, where it causes damage that could have been avoided.

Saying *I'm not sure* when you're not sure isn't a statement about your character. It's accurate information given to someone who needs accurate information to plan their life.

Tyler didn't figure this out by thinking about it. He figured it out by watching Priya look tired on presentation day and understanding the chain that led there.
