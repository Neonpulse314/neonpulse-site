Maya was 22, six months into her first real job, when her manager pulled her aside after a team meeting.

"You over-explain," he said. "When someone asks you a question, you give them the answer, then you give them three more answers in case they meant something else. People tune out by the second one. Just answer what they asked."

Maya nodded. She agreed. She thought he was right. On the walk home she replayed the meeting in her head and could see herself doing it — the way she'd hedged her main point with two alternates, the way she'd kept talking past the moment everyone had already gotten it.

She felt good about the conversation. She had heard the feedback. She would do better.

The next Tuesday she over-explained.

---

Here is what's strange. She did not forget. She did not disagree on reflection. If you had stopped her halfway through the over-explanation and asked, "Maya, what did your manager tell you last week?" she would have said it back word for word. The correction was there. It was filed. It was accessible.

It just wasn't operating.

This happens to people all the time, and most of us don't have a word for what it is. We say "I know but I keep doing it anyway" as if that's a complete sentence. It isn't. It points at something that has structure, and the structure is worth understanding, because not understanding it is how people stay stuck for years thinking they've already made the change.

The simplest way to describe it: when you hear a correction, two things have to happen for the correction to take. The first is that you have to receive it. The second is that the new way has to replace the old way as the default. The first is what feels like understanding. The second is the actual update.

You can do the first without the second. You can hear feedback, understand it, agree with it, and still leave the original behavior in the default slot. Then when the next situation arrives, the original behavior fires — because that's what's in the default slot — and the correction sits next to it like a note in the margin. Available, but not active.

---

Maya started to notice the gap. The third time she over-explained after the conversation, she watched herself doing it. Halfway through her second redundant clarification she caught it, finished the sentence with a wince, and went quiet.

This is the moment most people misread. They think: I know now, I'm self-aware, the change is happening. But noticing afterward isn't the change. The change is when the original behavior stops firing. Until then, all the noticing in the world is just better-quality regret.

What worked for Maya, eventually, wasn't more understanding. It was structure. She started writing her answer down in one sentence before speaking, on a notepad, under the table if she had to. The rule was: say what's on the page, then stop and let the silence sit for three seconds. The three seconds was the hard part. Her default was to fill silence by elaborating, and the structure gave her a place to put her hands instead.

After a month, the structure stopped being necessary. The new way had moved into the default slot. The original behavior was still in there — she could feel it sometimes, the urge to add the second clarification — but it wasn't firing first anymore.

---

The cost of confusing hearing with updating is that people spend years thinking they've already done the work. They hear the feedback, they internalize it, they tell themselves they've grown. And then they keep doing the thing, and the people around them stop trusting that feedback registers, because the evidence keeps saying it doesn't.

It also gets in the way of asking for more help. When you think you've understood and you keep failing, the failure feels like a character flaw — you knew and you still did it, so what does that say about you? But it doesn't say what you think it says. It says that knowing and updating are different operations, and you stopped after the first one.

The honest version of "I know but I keep doing it anyway" is: I received the correction, but I haven't installed it as the default yet. That's a different problem. It has different solutions. Most of them involve constraint, structure, or a way to slow yourself down at the moment the original behavior would otherwise fire.

What it asks of you isn't more understanding. It's the willingness to admit that understanding alone doesn't move you, and to set up the conditions that will.

---

*Hearing a correction is the easy part. Letting it replace what it was supposed to replace is what change actually is.*
