Marcus had been working on the same report for three days.

Not because the data was hard to find. Because every time he'd asked his manager a clarifying question in the past, he'd gotten an answer like "probably around Q3" or "I think we usually use the last two years" or "make it look like the ones we did before." He'd built the whole thing on approximations, and now nothing lined up.

He wasn't lazy. Neither was his manager. But somewhere in the middle, precision had gotten treated as optional — and now the work was built on sand.

---

Precision gets confused with pedantry. Pedantry is correcting someone's word choice when it doesn't change anything. Precision is making sure that when something matters, you know exactly what you mean.

The difference shows up in what happens next. Pedantry is about being right in the moment. Precision is about what you can build afterward.

If you say "I was kind of stressed last week," that's enough for casual conversation. If you're trying to figure out why your work has been slipping, or what you actually need, or whether a relationship is okay — vague descriptions produce vague diagnoses. You'll feel like you're thinking about the problem without actually narrowing it down.

---

Precision has two parts most people only practice one of.

The first is accuracy: getting the fact right, the number right, the word that actually matches what you mean. Most people know this is the goal. They try to be accurate.

The second is knowing your uncertainty: being clear about what you know precisely and what you're estimating. This is harder. It requires you to hold two things at once — the answer and the confidence level. Most people skip this part. They either state everything as certain or hedge everything equally, and in both cases the person listening can't tell which pieces to trust.

When you're precise about uncertainty — "I'm confident it was Q3, but I'd need to check whether we close the books on the 30th or 31st" — you give someone something they can actually work with. They know where to press and where they can trust the ground.

---

The cost of imprecision isn't usually one big mistake. It's a tax.

You have a conversation, but you're not quite sure what was agreed to, so you do something and then redo it. You get feedback that's hard to act on because it's too vague to know what to fix. You make a decision based on a number that turned out to be a rough estimate, and the error compounds. None of these feel catastrophic in the moment. They add up.

The thing that makes this hard to notice is that approximate answers feel just as good as exact ones when you hear them. The imprecision only shows up downstream, when you try to build something on top of it and the foundation shifts.

---

Marcus rebuilt the report. It took an afternoon instead of three days because he'd learned to ask different questions.

Not "what time period should I use?" but "is it 24 months back from today, or from the end of last fiscal year?" Not "how should it look?" but "can you send me the last version you were happy with?"

His manager hadn't changed. The questions had. And the questions changed because Marcus stopped treating approximation as good enough when it wasn't.

---

Being precise doesn't mean being slow or fussy. It means knowing which things actually need a real answer and making sure those things get one.

*Approximate answers are fine for approximate purposes. The trouble is forgetting which kind you're building on.*
