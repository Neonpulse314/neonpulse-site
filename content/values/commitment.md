Priya applied to six graduate programs and got into two.

Both were good. One was closer to home, one was the better school. She spent three weeks trying to decide, then chose the better school — and spent the first semester telling herself she might transfer. She researched the other program. She kept in touch with the admissions office just in case. She went to class, but part of her was still deciding.

By winter break, she'd made neither choice fully. She was at the better school but not in it.

---

There's a kind of math people do when they're afraid of commitment. You keep your options open, stay loosely attached to multiple paths, and tell yourself you're being strategic. What you're actually doing is paying a tax on everything.

The tax isn't obvious. You're still showing up. You're still doing the work. But half your energy goes toward maintaining the exit — toward the part of your mind that's still shopping for a better option, still calculating what it would cost to leave, still keeping the story flexible enough that you haven't fully committed to any version of it.

This is what commitment costs when you don't make it: not a dramatic failure, but a steady drain. A version of yourself that's always slightly somewhere else.

---

The word "commitment" has a trap in it. People think it means dedication — working hard, staying late, caring a lot. But those things can exist without commitment. You can care deeply about ten things and still be uncommitted to all of them.

Commitment is the act of closing doors. It means choosing this path in a way that makes the other paths unavailable — or at least making them feel that way, which amounts to the same thing psychologically. It's the decision that says: I'm going to find out what this becomes, and I'm going to find that out *here*.

What it costs you is optionality. The permanent sense that you're still deciding disappears, and with it the safety of never being wrong. If you commit and it goes badly, you can't say you were only half-in. You were there, and it went badly. That's a harder thing to sit with.

What it gives you is depth.

---

Priya knew a guy in her program — Kaito — who had gotten into only one school. He hadn't been able to transfer even if he wanted to. He'd had no choice but to be there, and so he was. He learned the professors' research. He found the seminars interesting. He built something real in two years.

She used to feel sorry for him. By spring she'd started to understand that he had something she didn't.

The difference wasn't that his program was better or that he worked harder. It was that he was actually in it. His energy wasn't split. He hadn't spent four months maintaining a mental escape route.

---

The fear underneath non-commitment is usually one of two things. The first is the fear of wasted time — what if this is wrong, and I'm stuck? The second is the fear of identity — what if committing to this means I'm *only* this?

Both fears are real but overweighted.

If you commit and it turns out wrong, you can change course. Most commitments are not prison sentences. The difference between a committed choice and an uncommitted one isn't whether you can leave — it's whether you gave the thing a real chance before you decided. Uncommitted people often leave before they've been in long enough to know whether it was working.

The identity fear is more interesting. Committing to something *does* narrow who you can be. A committed musician isn't equally available to become a committed programmer and a committed chef and a committed athlete at the same time. That's just true. But the narrowing is also how you become someone. Identity isn't what you're open to. It's what you've actually built.

---

Priya stopped researching the transfer process in February. She started going to the optional seminars. She introduced herself to a professor whose work she'd been vaguely interested in for months.

She didn't stop wishing the other school was closer to home. She just stopped using that wish as a reason not to be fully here.

The last semester was the only one that felt like hers.

---

*Commitment isn't the end of your options. It's the beginning of finding out what one of them actually is.*
