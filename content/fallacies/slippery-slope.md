The school board was debating whether to allow students to submit assignments digitally instead of on paper.

A parent stood up: "If we allow that, students will stop writing by hand entirely. Then they'll never develop fine motor skills. Then handwriting will disappear from the curriculum. Then we'll have a generation that can't fill out a paper form. Then we'll have people who can't function when the power goes out."

The proposal was about turning in homework digitally. It had traveled, in four steps, to societal collapse during a blackout.

---

## What It Is

The slippery slope fallacy claims that because one thing happens, a series of increasingly extreme consequences will inevitably follow — without providing evidence that the chain of events is likely, or even possible.

The structure looks like reasoning: A leads to B, B leads to C, C leads to D. But the logical work is hidden in the word "leads to." Each step is asserted, not demonstrated. The slope only works if each transition is actually probable, and slippery slope arguments usually skip over that entirely.

It's not that causal chains don't exist. They do. The fallacy is assuming the chain is both inevitable and catastrophic without making the case that either is true.

## A Clear Example

Priya's team was considering a flexible start time policy — employees could begin their workday anywhere between 7am and 10am.

Her manager's objection: "If we let people choose their start times, next they'll want to choose their end times too. Then they'll want to work from home whenever they feel like it. Then no one will actually come into the office. Then team cohesion collapses and we lose the culture that makes this place work."

Each step might be worth discussing individually. But the manager hadn't argued that any of them would actually happen — only that they could, strung together into a chain that ended somewhere alarming.

The proposal was flexible start times. The manager argued against a future with no office culture.

## When the Slope Is Real

Not every causal chain is a fallacy. Some slopes are real, and distinguishing them from slippery slope reasoning matters.

A real slope has two things:

**Evidence that the transitions are likely.** If lowering the voting age to 16 is likely to produce pressure to lower it to 14, there should be some reason to believe that — historical precedent, political dynamics, actual advocacy groups pushing for it. "It could happen" isn't enough.

**Each step being shown to lead to the next, not just asserted.** If someone can demonstrate that granting one exception to a policy reliably creates pressure for more exceptions — and can show data or historical examples — then the causal chain has support.

The difference is between "here's why this is likely" and "I'm asking you to imagine a bad outcome without showing how we get there."

## Why It Works

The slippery slope is effective because it shifts the conversation. Instead of debating the actual proposal — flexible start times, digital homework, whatever it is — you're suddenly defending yourself against the worst possible version of the downstream world. That's much harder to argue against, because you can't prove a negative about the future.

It also works because some slopes really are slippery, which gives the fallacy cover. People have seen cases where small precedents led to larger changes. That experience makes the pattern feel credible even when it isn't applied carefully.

The result: a proposal that might be good, neutral, or worth discussing on its own terms gets buried under catastrophizing about step seven in a chain that hasn't been shown to be real.

## What to Do When You Hear It

Ask for the mechanism. Not "what could happen" but "why would each step lead to the next, and what's the evidence?"

If the person making the argument can't show why step one leads to step two — specifically, not just imaginatively — the slope isn't doing any logical work. It's asking you to fear a destination without justifying the journey.

The actual question is still: is the proposal a good idea? That's the one worth answering.

## Related Fallacies

**Appeal to nature** — assumes that what's "natural" is better; similar in that it involves asserting a connection (natural = good) without evidence.

**False dichotomy** — presents only two options, often skipping middle ground; sometimes used alongside slippery slope to make "not doing X" seem like the only safe choice.
