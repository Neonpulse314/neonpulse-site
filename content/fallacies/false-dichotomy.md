The proposal had been on the table for twenty minutes when Marcus cut it short.

"Look, we either launch in Q2 or we lose the market window entirely. Those are the options."

Nobody pushed back. Q2 was tight but possible. Losing the market window sounded catastrophic. The team voted to launch in Q2.

Three weeks later, a competitor launched in Q3 — and did fine. The market window, it turned out, extended through summer. A Q3 launch with another six weeks of testing would have caught the integration bug that cost them four enterprise clients.

The third option had existed the whole time. It just hadn't been in the room.

---

## What It Is

The false dichotomy — also called false dilemma or either/or fallacy — presents two options as if they're exhaustive when other possibilities exist.

The structure is: *"It's either A or B."* But the actual choice space includes C, D, a combination of A and B, or something entirely different that hasn't been named yet.

Sometimes the dichotomy is deliberate — a rhetorical move to force a choice between a preferred option and an unappealing one. Sometimes it's accidental — the speaker genuinely hasn't noticed the middle ground. Either way, the effect is the same: options get eliminated before they're examined.

Common forms:
- "You're either with us or against us."
- "We either act now or do nothing."
- "Either we cut costs or we accept failure."
- "You can have it fast, or you can have it good."

Each of these might be *sometimes* true. When they're presented as *always* true — as the complete structure of the choice — they've become a fallacy.

---

## Why It Works

Binary choices are cognitively easier than complex ones. When you're offered two options, your brain can evaluate them, compare them, and pick. When you're offered six options with conditional dependencies, that's work. The false dichotomy is persuasive partly because it makes the decision feel tractable.

There's also a pressure dynamic. Marcus's framing — "Q2 or we lose the window" — created urgency. Urgency narrows attention. Under time pressure, people stop scanning for options and start evaluating the ones in front of them. The fallacy exploits this: once a binary is accepted, it's hard to reopen.

Psychologist Barry Schwartz documented the opposite problem — the paradox of choice, where too many options produce paralysis. The false dichotomy is the deliberate overcorrection: reduce choices below the real number, and people decide quickly and confidently, even when the decision is based on incomplete information.

---

## How to Spot It

**The "only" tell.** Watch for: "we only have two options," "it comes down to X or Y," "there's no middle ground here." These phrases often signal that the speaker has stopped looking for alternatives, not that alternatives don't exist.

**Both options share a hidden assumption.** False dichotomies often frame two options that both accept an unstated premise. "Either we raise prices or we cut staff" assumes the company can't grow revenue, find efficiencies, or restructure debt. The third option may live outside the frame entirely.

**Extreme poles.** If the two options presented are unusually far apart — act now vs. do nothing, total commitment vs. complete withdrawal — the dichotomy is probably false. Reality usually has a gradient between extremes.

**The urgency signal.** When a binary is combined with time pressure, slow down rather than speed up. Urgency is often real, but it's also the condition under which false dichotomies do their most damage. The narrower your attention, the more options you miss.

---

## What It Costs

The obvious cost is bad decisions — launching too early, missing the third option, shipping the bug.

The less obvious cost is to the decision-making culture. A team that regularly accepts false dichotomies stops generating alternatives. People learn that the framing of a choice determines the outcome, and they spend their energy fighting for their preferred framing rather than expanding the option space. The problem-solving muscle atrophies.

There's also a trust cost. When Marcus's Q2 framing turned out to be wrong, the team noticed. Not the decision — decisions can fail for good reasons. The framing. They had been given two options and told those were all that existed. They weren't. That kind of thing accumulates.

---

## What To Do About It

When you hear a binary framed as exhaustive, the move is simple: ask for the third option.

*"What would a Q3 launch look like? Is that actually off the table, or just not what we've been considering?"*

You're not arguing that the binary is wrong. You're asking whether it's complete. That's a harder question to dismiss — it doesn't attack the framing directly, it just asks it to justify itself.

When you catch yourself constructing a binary, ask: what am I not including? What assumptions am I making about what's possible? What does the space between these two options look like?

Binary thinking isn't always wrong. Some choices really are either/or. But the test should be *evidence* that the options are exhaustive — not just the way the choice happens to be sitting in your head right now.

---

Three weeks after the launch, Marcus ran a retrospective.

He didn't frame it as "we made the wrong call." He framed it as "we closed the option space too early."

That was the more useful failure. Not the decision — the process that produced it.

The team added one item to their decision checklist: *before voting, name at least one option that isn't on the table.*

It's a small thing. It's enough.
