On the night of 18 August 1913, at the Monte Carlo Casino, a roulette wheel started landing on black. Then it did it again. And again.

By the time it had come up black ten or twelve times running, a crowd had gathered around the table. The logic in the room was airtight, or felt like it: a run this long *cannot* continue. Red is overdue. People began pushing money onto red — and when black came again, they pushed more, convinced the correction was now even closer.

The wheel landed on black **twenty-six times in a row.** The casino took a fortune that night, not from the house edge but from people betting larger and larger sums against a streak that owed them nothing. By the time red finally came, most of the red-bettors had already been cleaned out.

The mistake they made has a name, and it's one of the easiest in the world to make.

## The wheel has no memory

Here is the whole trick, and it's almost insultingly simple: **each spin is independent.** The wheel does not know what it did last time. On a European wheel there are 37 pockets — eighteen red, eighteen black, one green zero — so the chance of red on any given spin is 18/37, about 48.6%. That number is the same on the first spin and the twenty-seventh. The previous twenty-six blacks change it by exactly nothing.

The gambler's fallacy is the belief that a random process *self-corrects in the short run* — that a run of one outcome makes the other "due." It feels true because we know, correctly, that things even out over time. Flip a fair coin a million times and you'll get close to half heads. So after five heads, tails feels owed.

But look closely at what "evening out" actually does. The chance of *twenty-six blacks in a row*, predicted before the first spin, is astronomically small — roughly **1 in 137 million.** That rarity is real. The error is applying it to the *next* spin. Once twenty-five blacks have already happened, the improbable part is already spent. The twenty-sixth spin is just a spin: 51% black, like always. The unlikely event doesn't have to be paid back, because there is no ledger. No accountant in the felt is tracking the debt.

The law of large numbers is real, but it doesn't work the way the crowd hoped. It doesn't *reverse* a streak — it *dilutes* it. Twenty-six blacks become statistically invisible not because red comes roaring back to balance the books, but because they get buried under millions of later spins that wash the average back toward half. The streak isn't repaid. It's drowned.

## How it shows up when there's no roulette wheel in sight

You don't need a casino to make this mistake. You need only an independent process and a human looking for a pattern in it.

- *"This team has lost five straight — they're due for a win."* Maybe they're due; maybe they're just worse than you thought. The losing streak is evidence for the second reading at least as much as the first.
- *"I've played these lottery numbers for ten years without winning, so I'm getting closer."* You are not. Each draw starts from zero.
- *"It's rained all week, we're owed a nice weekend."* The atmosphere is not keeping score.

The most expensive version is the **betting system.** The classic is the Martingale: after every loss, double your bet, so that a single win recovers everything plus a little. On paper it's unbeatable. In reality it's the gambler's fallacy wearing a suit. It assumes two things that aren't true — that a win is becoming more likely (it isn't), and that you have unlimited money and no table limit to ride out a bad run. A long enough losing streak *will* happen, and when it does it doesn't just cost you a bet. It costs you everything you've staked climbing the doubling ladder.

And here's the part worth sitting with. Even a player who *never* commits the gambler's fallacy still loses, because the game is tilted before anyone sits down — the green zero on the wheel, the vig on a sports bet, the spread the bookmaker bakes into both sides. The fallacy doesn't create the loss. It just convinces you to bet bigger into a game that was already against you, so you reach the bottom faster. The math was never waiting to reward your patience.

## When the streak actually means something

The fallacy is specifically about **independent** events. Not every streak is meaningless.

If you're dealt cards from a single deck and five aces' worth of high cards are already gone, the odds for the next card genuinely change — the past constrains the future. That's why card counting is real and casinos throw you out for it. And sometimes a long run is simply telling you your model is wrong: a coin that lands heads thirty times in a row is far more likely to be a weighted coin than a fair one having a wild day. There, the streak is evidence — about the coin, not about what's "due."

The skill is telling the two situations apart: is the past *changing* the next outcome, or just sitting in your memory making you feel owed?

The crowd in Monte Carlo felt owed. The wheel didn't agree. The dice have no debt, the ball has no memory, and the only thing that's ever truly *due* is the house — patiently, on every single spin.
