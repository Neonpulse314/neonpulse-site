The project had been running for seven months. The original deadline was March. It was now October. The feature it was building had been redesigned three times. Two engineers had quit. The product manager who commissioned it had moved to another company.

In a meeting to discuss whether to continue, the lead developer laid it out: "I know it's been rough. But we've put seven months into this. We can't just walk away from all of that."

Nobody challenged her. Seven months is a long time. Walking away felt like throwing it all away.

But here's what "all of that" actually meant: the seven months were already gone. Whether the project continued or stopped, those months weren't coming back. The only decision on the table was whether to spend additional time and money. The past investment had nothing to do with that question.

---

## What It Is

The sunk cost fallacy is the mistake of continuing an investment — of time, money, effort, or emotion — because of what has already been spent, rather than because of what continuing will actually produce.

A sunk cost is a cost that has already been incurred and cannot be recovered. In economics, the principle is clear: sunk costs are irrelevant to future decisions. The only thing that matters is whether the next dollar, hour, or unit of effort spent will generate enough future value to justify it.

The fallacy enters when past spending becomes a reason to keep going. "We've already spent $2 million on this" is not an argument for spending more. It's a description of the past. The relevant question is always: given where we are now, does continuing make sense?

## A Cleaner Example

Keiko bought a ticket to a concert for $80. The night of the show, she's exhausted and doesn't want to go. She forces herself to go anyway — "I already paid for it."

The $80 is gone whether she goes or not. It's not recoverable by attending. The actual choice she's making is between a night of rest and a night at a concert she doesn't want to attend. The price of the ticket is irrelevant to that choice — it just doesn't feel that way.

This is the core of the fallacy: the loss of the sunk cost feels like it will be made worse by not continuing, when in reality the decision is entirely about what happens next.

## Why It Persists

The sunk cost fallacy is hard to override because it's connected to real psychological forces.

**Loss aversion.** Stopping feels like confirming a loss. Continuing gives the illusion that the loss might still be recovered. In reality, the loss is fixed — but continuing to invest makes it feel provisional.

**Commitment and consistency.** People who have publicly committed to a project, a position, or a purchase feel social pressure to remain consistent with that commitment. Changing course requires admitting that the original decision was wrong.

**The narrative of waste.** "Don't quit" and "finish what you start" are taught as virtues. The sunk cost fallacy hijacks those values: quitting *feels* like waste, even when quitting is the rational choice.

These forces are real. The fallacy isn't a simple cognitive error — it's a systematic response to actual social and psychological pressures. Which is why it's so durable.

## The Actual Calculation

The correct question is not: "How much have we already spent?"

It's: "If we start fresh from today, with no history, is this worth pursuing?"

If the answer is no — if the project, investment, or situation wouldn't be worth starting from zero — then past investment is making you stay in something you wouldn't choose if you were choosing freely.

The developer in that meeting had framed the question wrong. The question wasn't whether seven months of work would be "wasted" if they stopped. It was: given where the product is today, is there a reasonable path to something worth shipping — and is that worth another three, six, or twelve months of effort?

That's a question the past can't answer.

## When Persistence Is Right

The sunk cost principle is sometimes misread as "always quit early" or "past investment is worthless." That's not right.

Long-term investments sometimes require patience through difficult stretches that look like failure. The question isn't whether something is hard or has had setbacks. The question is whether the future value justifies the future cost, evaluated as honestly as possible.

The mistake is letting "we've already come this far" do work it can't do. The past is evidence — it tells you something about the difficulty of the problem, the quality of the team, and the nature of the obstacle. But it doesn't make the path forward good. Only the future does that.

## Related Fallacies

**Escalation of commitment** — closely related pattern where decision-makers increase investment in a failing course of action; often studied in organizational behavior and public policy.

**Appeal to tradition** — argues that something is right because it has always been done; similar in treating history as justification for continuation.

**Hasty generalization** — can interplay with sunk cost when people generalize from "this has been hard" to "this must be worth it."
