Yusuf had been arguing with his roommate for twenty minutes about whether their landlord was trustworthy.

"Look," his roommate said, "he told us himself — he always keeps his word. So obviously we can trust what he says."

Yusuf paused. "Wait. You're saying we should trust him because he said he's trustworthy?"

"Exactly."

That was the whole argument. The landlord's word was the proof that his word was reliable. The conclusion was sitting inside the premise, dressed up as evidence.

---

## What It Is

Circular reasoning — also called begging the question — occurs when an argument uses its conclusion as one of its premises. The reasoning appears to make a case, but when you trace it back, the "support" for the claim is just the claim itself in different words.

The structure: "X is true because of Y, and Y is true because of X." Or more compactly: "X is true because X is true" — with enough rewording in between that the loop isn't immediately obvious.

It's not always dishonest. Sometimes people genuinely don't notice they've gone in a circle. The loop can be wide enough that the repetition gets lost.

## A Clear Example

A hiring manager is defending her decision to only interview candidates from a short list of universities.

"These schools produce the best graduates," she says.

"How do you know?" asks a colleague.

"Because the people we've hired from there have been excellent."

"And how did you decide they were excellent?"

"They graduated from top schools."

The argument is: graduates from these schools are excellent because they graduated from these schools. The definition of "excellent" includes the very thing being used as evidence of excellence. There's no independent measure.

## Why It's Hard to Catch

Circular reasoning is harder to spot than most fallacies because it can hide inside long, complex arguments. If the loop spans several steps — A proves B, B proves C, C proves A — each individual link might look valid. It's only when you map the whole chain that you see it doubles back.

Language also helps it hide. A conclusion and its restatement rarely sound identical. "He's honest" and "he always tells the truth" feel like two different things, even though one is just a paraphrase of the other. The rephrasing creates the illusion of movement.

It also survives because it's unfalsifiable. If the only evidence for a claim is the claim itself, there's no outside standard to test against. That insulation can feel like strength rather than weakness.

## What's Actually Needed

A valid argument needs premises that are independent of the conclusion — evidence or reasons that could be true even if the conclusion turned out to be false.

To break the landlord example: independent evidence would be a track record of actually honoring specific past commitments, other tenants' testimony, documentation. Not "he said so."

The hiring manager example: independent evidence would be performance data for employees from those schools versus others, controlling for role and time period. Not "we hired them because they went there, and they were good because we hired them."

The question to ask: if the conclusion were false, would the premises still hold? If not — if believing the premises requires already accepting the conclusion — the argument is circular.

## The Soft Version

Not all circular reasoning is a closed loop. A softer version appears when someone defines a term in a way that makes their conclusion true by definition, then treats that definition as a discovery.

"True art must move people. This painting doesn't move people. Therefore it isn't true art."

The conclusion was built into the premise. There's no room for the argument to fail, because "true art" was defined as exactly the thing being argued. This is circular reasoning embedded in a definition — harder to catch, but the same structure underneath.

## Related Fallacies

**Appeal to authority** — instead of looping through the same claim, it outsources the proof to someone who is assumed to be right; if that assumption isn't justified, the authority's word is just another ungrounded premise.

**Straw man** — misrepresents the opponent's argument; different in that it attacks a distorted version of the claim rather than looping back through the original.
