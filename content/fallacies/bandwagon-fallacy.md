The product team had been debating the feature for two weeks when Marcus finally said it.

"Look — our three biggest competitors all have this. Our users are asking for it. Everyone in this space is moving this direction. At some point we just have to accept that this is where the market is going."

Nobody pushed back. It felt like the argument had been made.

But it hadn't. The question wasn't whether other companies had the feature. The question was whether the feature would make their product better. Those are different questions, and Marcus had only answered the first one.

---

## What It Is

The bandwagon fallacy — also called *ad populum*, or appeal to the people — argues that because many people do, believe, or support something, that thing is correct, good, or worth doing.

The structure: *X is popular, therefore X is right.*

Popularity and correctness are not the same thing. A belief held by millions can still be wrong. A practice widespread across an industry can still be a mistake. The number of people doing something tells you nothing about whether it's a good idea — only that it's common.

## A Clearer Example

Dani was trying to decide whether to put her savings into a particular investment. Her brother kept sending her articles about how many people were buying in.

"It's everywhere," he said. "Everyone's talking about it. You don't want to be the last one."

Dani asked: "But what's the actual case for it? What's the return mechanism?"

Her brother didn't have an answer. The argument was entirely social — other people were doing it, so she should too.

The popularity of an investment doesn't make it sound. If it did, bubbles wouldn't be possible. Every asset bubble in history involves a point where nearly everyone believed prices would keep rising. The crowd wasn't right. It was just large.

## When Consensus Is Legitimate Evidence

The bandwagon fallacy is easy to misapply in the other direction. It doesn't mean that popular positions are always wrong, or that majority opinion is worthless.

There's a real difference between *popular belief* and *expert consensus*. When the overwhelming majority of climate scientists, based on decades of evidence and peer review, agree that the climate is warming and humans are causing it — that's not a bandwagon. That's a field reaching a conclusion through a rigorous process.

The relevant questions are:

**How was the consensus formed?** A consensus that emerged from independent researchers testing each other's work over decades is different from one that emerged from social pressure, marketing, or people repeating what they heard.

**Are the people agreeing qualified to evaluate the claim?** A hundred strangers on the internet agreeing that a medical treatment works is different from three hundred independent clinical trials showing the same thing.

**What would it take to change the consensus?** If the answer is "more evidence," the consensus is scientific. If the answer is "nothing — it's just what everyone knows," it's a bandwagon.

## Why It Works

The bandwagon is persuasive because social belonging is a real and powerful motivator. Humans have evolved to track what the group is doing — in most environments, following the group is safer than going it alone. That instinct doesn't turn off when the question is whether a product feature is a good idea.

It also works because popularity really is *some* evidence — just not the kind people usually imply. If millions of customers use a product, that tells you something. The problem is when that data point gets overextended to answer questions it can't answer: not just "do people use this?" but "is this right?" or "should we do this?"

The feature Marcus was pushing might have been a good idea. The problem was that he'd stopped trying to argue it was, and started arguing that it was inevitable. Those are different things.

## Related Fallacies

**Appeal to authority** — argues that a claim is true because an authority figure says so; similar in that it outsources the reasoning rather than providing it.

**Hasty generalization** — draws broad conclusions from too small a sample; can overlap with bandwagon when small trends get treated as universal.

**False dichotomy** — sometimes used alongside bandwagon to suggest that not following the crowd means being left behind, with no middle ground presented.
