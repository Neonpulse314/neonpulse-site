Priya's coworker was explaining why she'd switched to a homeopathic cold remedy.

"I just don't want to put chemicals in my body," she said. "This is all-natural ingredients. Nothing artificial."

Priya thought about it. Water is natural. Arsenic is natural. Botulinum toxin — the most acutely toxic substance known — is completely natural. Penicillin was derived from mold. Aspirin was originally derived from willow bark. The synthetic version and the natural version are chemically identical.

"What does natural mean, exactly?" she asked.

Her coworker didn't have a clear answer.

---

## What It Is

The appeal to nature is the assumption that something is good, safe, or correct because it's natural — and correspondingly, that something is bad, unsafe, or wrong because it's artificial, synthetic, or human-made.

It appears in arguments like:
- "It's all-natural, so it must be safe"
- "That's artificial — I'd rather have the natural version"
- "Humans evolved to eat this way, so it must be healthier"
- "It's a chemical — can't be good for you"

The error is treating "natural" and "artificial" as meaningful quality distinctions. They describe origin, not safety, efficacy, or ethical weight.

## Why Natural Doesn't Mean Safe

Nature produces some of the most toxic substances known:
- **Ricin**, from castor beans — so toxic that a microgram can kill
- **Botulinum toxin**, from bacteria — the most acutely lethal substance per unit weight
- **Arsenic**, a naturally occurring element — a classic poison
- **Cyanide**, found in apple seeds, cherry pits, and bitter almonds

Meanwhile, many beneficial substances are synthetic:
- **Aspirin** (acetylsalicylic acid) was synthesized to improve on the natural compound in willow bark — it's more effective and better tolerated
- **Insulin** for diabetics was once extracted from animal pancreases (natural); now most is produced synthetically (artificial) — and the synthetic version is purer and more consistent
- **Vaccines** use killed or weakened pathogens, or synthesized proteins — some "natural," some not, all evaluated on whether they work and are safe

The word "chemical" is particularly telling. Everything is a chemical. Water (H₂O) is a chemical. Oxygen is a chemical. The framing of "chemicals vs. natural ingredients" doesn't describe a real distinction — it describes a feeling about familiarity.

## Why It's Compelling

The appeal to nature taps into something real: our evolutionary intuition that familiar things are safer than unfamiliar ones. For most of human history, novel substances *were* more likely to be dangerous. That intuition had survival value.

But it's a heuristic that breaks down in a world where both beneficial medicines and dangerous toxins come in "natural" and "artificial" versions. The relevant question is always: what does the evidence say about this specific substance? Not: where did it come from?

There's also a moving target problem. What counts as "natural" is never clearly defined. Cheese is fermented and processed. Bread requires heat transformation. Herbal supplements are extracted and concentrated. Most things people call "natural" involve significant processing — they're just processed in ways that feel less industrial.

## The Naturalistic Fallacy

Philosophers call the broader version of this the naturalistic fallacy: the error of equating "natural" with "good" in a moral sense. Whatever exists in nature is not automatically right. Natural predation, disease, and parasitism exist. Evolution is not optimizing for human wellbeing.

This matters in ethical arguments too. "It's natural for humans to eat meat" and "it's natural for men to be dominant" both use nature as a stand-in for justification. But what's natural in an evolutionary or biological sense has no direct bearing on what's ethical or optimal.

## What to Ask Instead

The right questions have nothing to do with natural vs. artificial:

- What does the evidence say about safety and efficacy?
- At what dose?
- For which population?
- Compared to what alternative?

A natural substance that's been tested and shown to be safe and effective is worth using. A synthetic substance that's been tested and shown to be safe and effective is also worth using. An untested substance — natural or synthetic — is a gamble regardless of its origin.

"Natural" is a marketing word. It describes where something came from, not whether it works or whether it's safe.

## Related Fallacies

**Slippery slope** — another argument that substitutes a feared outcome for evidence; both avoid engaging with the actual merits of a proposal.

**Appeal to tradition** — similar structure: assumes that old or traditional things are better because they've lasted, without asking whether they work.
