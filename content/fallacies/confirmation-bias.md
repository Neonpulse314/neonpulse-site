After three weeks of research, Jin had assembled a compelling case.

She'd read eight articles, watched four documentary segments, and joined two online communities. Every source she found pointed in the same direction. The evidence was consistent. Her original hunch had been right.

Her colleague looked at her list of sources and asked a simple question: "Did you look for anything that pushed back on this?"

Jin paused. She hadn't, not really. Every search she'd run was phrased to find support. Every community she'd joined was already convinced. The evidence was consistent because she'd built a consistent evidence pile — not because the question only had one answer.

## What It Is

Confirmation bias is the tendency to seek out, favor, interpret, and remember information that supports what you already believe — and to discount, ignore, or reinterpret information that challenges it.

The name was coined by psychologist Peter Wason, who demonstrated it in a 1960 experiment. Participants were given the sequence 2, 4, 6 and asked to discover the rule that generated it. They could propose any three numbers and would be told whether that sequence also fit the rule.

Most participants immediately hypothesized that the rule was "increasing even numbers" or "add 2 each time." Then they tested sequences that confirmed it — 8, 10, 12. Yes. 14, 16, 18. Yes. They called the rule confident they'd found it.

The actual rule was simply "any ascending numbers." Any sequence with each number larger than the last would work: 1, 2, 3. 5, 50, 500. 7, 100, 4,000.

Most participants never tested a disconfirming sequence. They tested hypotheses that would verify what they'd already guessed, and stopped when they got enough "yes" answers. The rule they found was real but narrow. The actual rule was broader, and they'd never looked for it.

## What It Looks Like in Practice

Confirmation bias isn't limited to people who are intellectually lazy or ideologically rigid. It affects careful, well-intentioned people too — which is part of what makes it difficult to catch.

A manager believes a particular employee is underperforming. In weekly check-ins, she notices every missed deadline, every ambiguous answer, every moment of uncertainty. She doesn't notice the deliverables that came in on time or the client compliments. The belief shaped what she attended to.

A person starts a new supplement regimen and begins to feel better after two weeks. They attribute it to the supplement. They don't consider that they changed three habits at the same time, or that two weeks is the natural resolution time for the mild fatigue they were experiencing, or that they were paying close attention to their health in ways they usually don't.

A student encounters a study that confirms something they were taught. They share it. They don't run the same check on the ten studies that came before it — only the one that matched.

In each case, the person isn't lying or being deliberately selective. They're processing information the way brains do: finding patterns, filling gaps, and building coherent narratives. Confirmation bias is partly how cognition works, not a failure of it.

## Why It's Hard to Catch

The difficulty with confirmation bias is that when it's operating, it doesn't feel like bias. It feels like research. The sources seem credible. The pattern seems clear. The conclusions feel earned.

This is different from motivated reasoning, where you know you want a particular outcome and consciously steer toward it. Confirmation bias operates before that level of awareness. The search terms you choose, the communities you join, the articles you click — these feel like neutral choices, not filtered ones.

It's also self-reinforcing. The more you read on a topic from a single direction, the more fluent you become in that perspective's language and evidence. You get better at finding sources. You start to recognize names and publications. The expertise feels like convergence on truth. It may just be familiarity with one quadrant of the map.

## How to Push Back on It

The most direct counter to confirmation bias is adversarial research: deliberately seeking the strongest case against your current position.

This isn't the same as looking for bad arguments to dismiss. It means finding the most credible, well-reasoned opposition and engaging with it seriously. What would someone who had read everything on this subject and reached the opposite conclusion argue? What evidence would they cite? Where does that evidence come from?

The goal isn't to change your mind on every question — it's to ensure that your position survives contact with its best opposition, not just its weakest.

A practical test: if you can't articulate the strongest objection to your own position in terms that the other side would recognize as fair, you probably haven't finished your research.

## The Wason Test at Scale

Jin's research wasn't wrong because she was careless. It was wrong because she'd treated evidence-gathering as confirmation-gathering. The sources she found were real. The pattern was real. She just hadn't asked the question that would break it.

The Wason experiment works on individuals in a lab. Confirmation bias at scale — entire industries, political movements, scientific subfields — works the same way, just slower and harder to notice. The sequence 2, 4, 6 fits a lot of rules. Finding which one is true requires testing what doesn't fit, not just what does.

## Related Fallacies

**Hasty generalization** — draws a broad conclusion from too few examples; often fueled by confirmation bias selecting which examples get counted.

**Availability heuristic** — overweights recent or memorable events; pairs with confirmation bias when vivid confirming examples feel more representative than they are.

**Appeal to authority** — sometimes used to lend weight to already-held beliefs; confirmation bias shapes which authorities get consulted.
