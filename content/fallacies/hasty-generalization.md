The first three contractors Marcus hired were unreliable. One showed up late, one left a job unfinished, and one charged him more than the original quote. After the third one, Marcus made a decision: he wasn't hiring contractors anymore.

His wife pointed out that they needed a plumber. Marcus said he'd just watch YouTube videos and figure it out. The flooded bathroom two weeks later didn't change his view about contractors — it just confirmed that doing things himself was the right call.

---

## What It Is

The hasty generalization draws a broad conclusion from a sample that is too small, too selective, or not representative of the larger group.

The structure: a few examples of X have property Y, therefore all (or most) X have property Y.

The problem isn't that the examples are false. Marcus's three bad contractors were real. The problem is that three cases — out of however many thousands of contractors exist — aren't enough to support a universal conclusion. The sample is too small, and it's not random: he stopped collecting data as soon as he had enough to justify the conclusion he'd already started forming.

## Why the Examples Feel Sufficient

Hasty generalizations don't feel like reasoning errors. They feel like pattern recognition — which they are. The cognitive machinery that produces them is the same machinery that lets humans learn from experience. If you touch a hot stove twice and get burned, generalizing "hot stoves burn" is correct and useful.

The trouble is that the same process fires regardless of sample size. Three bad experiences and three hundred bad experiences produce the same gut feeling of certainty. The emotion of having learned something doesn't scale with the evidence.

This is amplified by the fact that confirming examples are more memorable than disconfirming ones. Marcus remembered the three unreliable contractors clearly. He probably doesn't remember the plumber who showed up on time when he was a kid, or the electrician who stayed late to finish the job. Those didn't register as data points — they were just things that went fine.

## A Cleaner Example

Leila's team had a difficult time collaborating with the marketing department on one project. The timelines kept shifting, the briefs were vague, and the final deliverable got changed after everyone had signed off.

At the next all-hands, Leila said: "We've learned that marketing projects always go like this. We need to build in extra buffer any time we're working with them."

One project. One team. A conclusion about how marketing always operates.

The generalization might be correct — or the project might have been unusually chaotic for reasons that had nothing to do with marketing. The team lead who commissioned it might have been new. The product might have been changing under everyone. One project isn't enough to know.

## When Generalizing Is Legitimate

Not all generalizations from limited samples are fallacies. Some things are worth concluding quickly.

**When the mechanism is well-understood.** If you've seen the same failure mode in a software system twice and you understand exactly why it's happening, generalizing from two cases is reasonable. You're not just counting incidents — you're modeling a cause.

**When the cost of being wrong is low and the cost of waiting is high.** "That intersection seems dangerous — I'll take a different route" based on two near-misses is sensible. You're not claiming the intersection is objectively dangerous to all drivers forever; you're making a personal, reversible decision with limited stakes.

**When the sample is large and representative.** A clinical trial with 10,000 participants and random assignment produces generalizations that hold. Three contractors do not.

The difference is between "I have enough evidence to act on this" and "I have enough evidence to declare this universally true." The hasty generalization confuses the two.

## What to Do When You Spot One

Ask: how many cases? How were they selected? What would disconfirming evidence look like — and have you looked for it?

Marcus's three bad contractors were a reason to be careful about vetting contractors, not a reason to conclude that contractors are unreliable as a category. A different response: ask for references, check reviews, get a written quote. Use the bad experiences to build a better hiring process, not to close off the category entirely.

The generalization was the shortcut. The better answer was slower, and it required admitting that three cases wasn't a policy.

## Related Fallacies

**Confirmation bias** — the tendency to seek evidence that confirms existing beliefs; closely linked to hasty generalization, which often stops collecting evidence once a conclusion feels reached.

**Anecdotal evidence** — treating personal experience as equivalent to systematic data; the mechanism behind most hasty generalizations.

**Bandwagon fallacy** — sometimes hasty generalizations get treated as proof that "everyone knows" something, lending false authority to the conclusion.
