A theater in Ohio, 1984. Researchers sold season tickets to its real customers, and quietly randomized the price. Some people paid full freight, $15. Others got a small discount, or a large one — $7 off — handed to them at the window for no reason. Same seats, same plays, same season.

Then Hal Arkes and Catherine Blumer counted who actually showed up. Over the first half of the season, the people who'd paid full price attended noticeably more plays than the people who'd gotten a discount. Nothing about the plays differed. The only thing that differed was how much each person had spent to get in the door — and the more they'd spent, the more they dragged themselves out on a cold night to a show they might not have wanted to see.

That's the sunk cost fallacy, and it was published in *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes* in 1985. The full-price ticket holders weren't going because they'd enjoy it. They were going because not-going would mean the money was wasted — and that feeling, the researchers found, is what drives the whole thing: the desire not to look (even to yourself) like you threw money away.

## The trap is grammatical

A sunk cost is any investment — money, time, effort — that you've already made and can't get back. The fallacy is treating that past spending as a reason to keep going. And the reason it's so hard to see is that it hides inside a sentence that sounds responsible: *"I've already put so much into this."*

That sentence feels like diligence. It's actually a category error. The money for the ticket is gone whether you attend or not. The two years you spent on the wrong degree are gone whether you finish it or switch. The only thing your decision can affect is the *future* — the cold night ahead, the next two years — and the past spending has no information about whether the future is worth it. You're letting a number that can't change cast a vote on a choice that still can.

*The receipt can't vote. Only the road ahead can.*

## The test

There's a clean way to check yourself, and it costs nothing. Ask: *if I were deciding this fresh today — no history, no money already in — would I start?*

Would I buy a ticket to tonight's play at tonight's price? Would I sign up for this degree, this relationship, this project, knowing what I now know, if I were standing at the start with empty hands? If the answer is no, then the only thing keeping you in is the receipt — and the receipt is not the result. Finishing a bad thing doesn't refund the cost of it. It just adds tonight to the bill.

## What it actually costs

The reason this one is worth practicing is that the fallacy is expensive in exactly the way it pretends to be thrifty. Governments finished the Concorde — a beautiful airplane that never made money — partly because they'd already spent so much that stopping felt like admitting waste. The waste was real either way. Pouring in more only made it bigger.

The skill the sunk cost fallacy asks of you is not cleverness. It's the willingness to let a loss be a loss — to look at money or years you can't recover and *not* spend more chasing the feeling of having recovered them. That's harder than it sounds, because walking away feels like the wasteful choice. It's usually the opposite. The wasteful choice already happened. The only question left is how much more you're willing to add to it.
