The city council was reviewing a complaint about a construction company that had violated noise ordinances on a residential street — work that started at 5am, three days in a row.

The company's representative spoke up: "This company has been building in this city for thirty years. We've completed four hospitals, two schools, and the waterfront development. We employ two hundred people locally."

None of that addressed the noise ordinance violation. But several council members nodded. The complaint felt smaller.

---

## What It Is

A red herring is anything introduced into an argument that's irrelevant to the actual question — but presented in a way that makes it seem relevant. The goal, usually unconscious, is to move the conversation somewhere more favorable without the other side noticing the shift.

The name comes from an old fox-hunting training technique: dragging a smoked fish across a trail to confuse hunting dogs and train them to follow the real scent despite distractions. Whether or not the technique was actually used this way is disputed, but the metaphor stuck.

A red herring doesn't try to refute your argument. It just changes the subject. If it works, you end up defending the new subject instead of the original one, and the original question quietly disappears.

## A Clear Example

Theo was presenting a case to his team for switching to a different project management tool. He'd laid out cost savings, feature comparisons, and migration timelines.

His colleague responded: "Look, we've been using this tool since we were founded. It's part of how we work. Changing our tools changes who we are as a company."

The question was whether the new tool was better for the team's needs. His colleague introduced company identity and tradition — real topics, but not the relevant ones for evaluating two pieces of software. If Theo started defending change as a concept, or arguing about company culture, he'd already lost the thread.

## Why It's Hard to Catch

Red herrings work partly because the thing introduced is often genuinely interesting or emotionally resonant. Company history and identity are worth thinking about. A construction company's community contribution is real. These aren't lies — they're just not relevant to the specific question being answered.

The distraction also takes advantage of how conversations work. When someone raises a new point, social pressure pushes you to respond to it. Saying "that's not relevant" can feel dismissive or combative, even when it's accurate. So you engage, and the original argument gets left behind.

It's also easy to confuse red herrings with genuine context. Sometimes background information actually is relevant. The difference is whether it bears on the specific question being decided — whether it makes the answer more or less likely, or changes what the right answer is.

## Spotting It

The test is simple: does this new information change the answer to the original question?

If the construction company's thirty-year history explains why they might have a waiver for early start times — or why the council might want to weigh leniency — it's relevant. If it's just meant to make the complaint feel smaller without addressing it, it's a red herring.

You can name it without being combative: "That's worth knowing, but it doesn't address whether the noise ordinance was violated. Can we come back to that?" This returns the conversation to the original question without dismissing what was said.

## The Pattern in Practice

Red herrings appear most often when:

- Someone can't answer the original question directly
- The actual question puts them in a weak position
- Emotional appeals are more available than logical ones

Politicians use them constantly. "Yes but look at what the other party did" is a red herring when the question is about what this party did. Bringing up a candidate's personal life when the question is about policy is a red herring. So is pivoting to economic achievements when asked about a specific law.

In everyday arguments, they're usually less deliberate — people naturally drift toward ground they're more comfortable on. Recognizing the drift, and returning to the original question, is the skill.

## Related Fallacies

**Ad hominem** — attacks the person making the argument rather than the argument; related in that both avoid engaging with the actual claim.

**Straw man** — misrepresents the argument before attacking it; different in that it stays on the same topic but distorts it, while a red herring changes topics entirely.
