Marcus had been arguing climate policy with his uncle for twenty minutes when his uncle finally said: "You're twenty-two years old and you've never paid a mortgage. You don't get to lecture me about the economy."

The conversation stopped. Marcus didn't have a comeback. His uncle seemed to have won.

Except he hadn't answered a single point Marcus made.

## What Ad Hominem Actually Is

*Ad hominem* is Latin for "to the person." It describes any argument that attacks the person making a claim rather than the claim itself.

The structure is always the same: instead of engaging with what someone said, you find something about *them* that's unflattering or problematic, then imply that this invalidates their position.

Marcus's uncle didn't address whether Marcus's points about carbon pricing were correct or incorrect. He pointed at Marcus's age and financial inexperience. Whether those things are relevant to climate economics is a separate question — but they weren't offered as relevant evidence. They were offered as a reason to stop listening.

That's the move. Redirect attention from the argument to the arguer.

## Why It Works

Ad hominem is effective for several reasons.

First, it exploits a real psychological tendency: we do naturally evaluate claims partly through the credibility of the source. A doctor's advice on medication carries different weight than a stranger's. That instinct isn't wrong. But ad hominem hijacks it — instead of questioning *relevant* credentials, it questions anything unflattering.

Second, it puts the target on the defensive. Once Marcus is defending his age and financial history, he's no longer making the argument he came to make. He's been moved to different terrain, and the original points have been abandoned.

Third, it's often *partially* true. Marcus *is* twenty-two. He *hasn't* paid a mortgage. That truth makes the attack feel grounded even when it doesn't address the logic.

## The Three Common Forms

**Direct ad hominem** — straightforwardly insulting the person. "You're an idiot, so why would I listen to you?" The bluntest version. Easy to spot.

**Circumstantial ad hominem** — suggesting the person has a self-interested motive that invalidates their view. "Of course you support higher minimum wage — you work for minimum wage." The person's interest might be real, but having an interest in a position doesn't make the position wrong. Oil executives *and* environmental scientists both have interests in the climate debate. You still have to evaluate the evidence.

**Tu quoque** ("you too") — pointing out that the person doesn't follow their own position. "You tell me to exercise more but you haven't been to the gym in a year." Even if the accusation is true, it doesn't affect whether the advice is correct. A smoker who tells you not to smoke is still giving you good advice.

## When Credentials Actually Matter

This is where it gets careful: sometimes who is speaking *is* relevant.

If a person claims to be an eyewitness and they weren't present, that's relevant. If a scientist's study was funded by the company whose product the study evaluates, that's relevant — not because their conclusion is automatically wrong, but because it's legitimate to scrutinize the methodology more carefully.

The test is whether the personal fact *logically bears on the claim*. Not just whether it makes the person look bad.

"You've never run a business" is ad hominem if the topic is tax policy in general. It becomes potentially relevant if the specific argument is about what it feels like to manage payroll. Context matters.

The question to ask: *even if this unflattering thing about the person is true, does it give us any reason to think the argument is wrong?* If the answer is no, it's ad hominem.

## Back to Marcus

Marcus's uncle made a real rhetorical move. It was effective in the short term — it ended the conversation and left Marcus flustered.

But if Marcus had caught the fallacy in the moment, he had a clean response: "Whether I've paid a mortgage doesn't change whether the data on carbon pricing is accurate. What's wrong with the argument I'm making?"

That's the counter to ad hominem: return to the argument. Make them engage with the claim, not the claimant.

His uncle would then have had to either make a substantive response — or reveal that he didn't have one.

---

*Ad hominem is often easy to spot when it's aimed at someone you agree with. The harder skill is noticing it when it's aimed at someone you already dislike — because then it feels like a good point.*
